The short answer is once a year for most Treasure Valley homes, in spring.

The useful answer is that your lot matters far more than the calendar. Two identical houses three streets apart can need genuinely different schedules, and the difference is almost always about shade, water and what is upwind.

## What actually accumulates on a house here

Four things, and they behave differently.

**High-desert dust.** Constant through the dry months. It is inorganic, it does not grow, and it rinses off reasonably well. It is also relentless, and it works into textured surfaces like stucco far more than it does into smooth siding.

**Organic growth.** Algae and mildew on anything that stays damp. This is the one that genuinely needs treating rather than rinsing, because it has roots and it comes back from them.

**Hard water deposits.** From sprinkler overspray hitting siding and glass. Mineral, not dirt, and it builds up permanently until something dissolves it.

**Pollen and seed.** Seasonal, heavy in spring, mostly cosmetic.

A house with only the first and the fourth needs a wash. A house with the second and third needs a treatment, which is a different job.

## Once a year, and why spring

Spring works for most homes for three practical reasons.

Winter leaves the worst of it behind: grime that has sat since autumn, salt and road film near busy streets, and whatever the freeze-thaw cycle has driven into porous surfaces.

Doing it before the dry season starts means the house looks right through the months you are actually outside, and it gives you a clean baseline before the dust arrives.

And it pairs naturally with the other spring jobs: gutters after winter, concrete before sealing season, windows before you start opening them.

## When once a year is not enough

### You have significant tree cover

This is the biggest single factor. Shade keeps surfaces damp, damp grows algae, and trees drop organic debris that feeds it.

The mature canopy through the North End and Hyde Park is the clearest example in Boise. Old Town Meridian near Kleiner Park, the elm-shaded streets of the Caldwell historic districts, and Old Nampa all behave the same way. Those homes often want attention twice a year, and the north elevation usually goes first regardless of what the rest of the house looks like.

### You are near water

Homes backing the Boise River and the Greenbelt, through Harris Ranch and Barber Valley, and around the private lakes in Eagle communities like Two Rivers and Laguna Pointe, sit in ambient humidity that does not burn off the way it does on an open lot.

### Your sprinklers hit the house

Extremely common and almost entirely fixable. Overspray hitting siding and glass every morning through an irrigation season produces mineral staining that compounds year over year. Adjusting the heads is ten minutes of work and the highest-value maintenance decision on this list.

### You are on the agricultural edge

Homes out toward Lake Lowell, along the farm corridors, out past the Caldwell vineyards or on acreage near open ground deal with field dust and crop debris that suburban lots do not. The load is simply higher.

### You are near active construction

A lot of the valley is. Jobsite dust travels, and it is finer and stickier than ordinary dust.

### It is a commercial frontage

Different standard entirely. A storefront is usually quarterly or monthly, because it is the first thing a customer sees.

## When once a year is more than enough

Open lot, full sun on all elevations, no large trees, sprinklers correctly adjusted, not near farmland or construction. That describes a lot of newer South Meridian, South Nampa and outer subdivision housing.

Those homes genuinely can go eighteen months to two years between washes without looking neglected, and there is no sense paying for a schedule the property does not need.

## How to read your own house

Rather than guessing, go and look. Three checks.

**Walk the north side.** Whatever is going to happen to your house happens there first. If the north elevation has a green or grey film, or dark streaking under the eaves, you are overdue regardless of what the front looks like.

**Look at the sheltered spots.** Under eaves, behind downspouts, around the base of walls, anywhere that does not get direct sun or rain. Growth starts in the protected places.

**Check the glass near the sprinklers.** Cloudy spotting at the bottom of windows on the lawn side tells you overspray is hitting the house, and if it is on the glass it is on the siding too.

If all three look fine, you can wait. If the north side is going, book it.

## What waiting too long actually costs

The case is not purely cosmetic.

**Organic growth holds moisture against the building.** On wood siding and trim, prolonged dampness is how rot starts. On stucco, it works into the texture and becomes much harder to remove.

**Mineral staining becomes permanent.** Deposits that sit through repeated heat cycles bond harder, and on glass they eventually etch. The same stain is easy this year and may be permanent in five.

**Growth that establishes needs stronger treatment.** A light film is a straightforward soft wash. Years of accumulation needs more dwell time, more product and more care, which costs more.

**Paint and finishes degrade faster** under a layer that traps moisture against them.

The pattern across all of them is the same: regular is cheaper per visit than occasional, because you never let the expensive problems establish.

## What a house wash should actually involve

Worth knowing, because "house wash" covers a wide range.

A proper job is a **soft wash** on the siding, which means low pressure and a cleaning solution that kills organic growth rather than blasting it. High pressure on siding forces water behind panels and into the wall cavity, which is a far more expensive problem than a dirty house.

It should include the soffits, fascia and eaves, which is where growth hides and where most of the dark streaking lives.

Landscaping gets pre-wet and rinsed.

It usually makes sense to do the flatwork at the same visit, because the setup is shared and the driveway is what people actually look at on their way to the door.

## Siding material changes the answer

What your house is clad in affects both how often it needs washing and how it has to be done.

**Vinyl** is the most forgiving and the most common on newer builds. It does not absorb much, so dirt sits on the surface and releases easily. The risk is pressure, which forces water up behind the panels, because vinyl is designed to shed water downward and is not sealed at the laps.

**Stucco** is porous and textured, which means it holds dust in a way smooth siding does not, and it shows organic growth more obviously. It also cannot take pressure, because the texture chips. Stucco homes often look like they need washing sooner than they actually do, and they need a gentler approach when they do.

**Fibre cement** is durable and takes cleaning well, but the paint finish is the thing you are protecting, so soft washing is the right method.

**Wood and cedar** are the most demanding. They absorb moisture, they gouge under pressure, and the grain raises if they are treated roughly. Older homes through the North End, Hyde Park, Old Town Eagle and the historic Nampa and Caldwell districts frequently have original wood siding and trim that needs the gentlest handling on this list.

**Brick** is low maintenance but the mortar is not. Aggressive cleaning washes out soft or aged mortar joints, which is a repointing job.

## What this looks like through the year

**Late winter into spring.** The main event. House wash, gutters cleared of whatever winter left, windows, and the flatwork. This is also when you want to be booking concrete sealing, because the weather window for curing is narrow and it fills.

**Early summer.** Walk the irrigation zones and watch where the heads actually throw. This single habit prevents more staining than any cleaning schedule.

**Late summer.** The dust peak. Mostly a question of whether appearance matters to you between now and autumn. Commercial frontages usually need attention here.

**Autumn.** Gutters, before the first hard freeze, and this window is tighter than people expect. Roof cleaning if it is due, while the weather still cooperates.

**Winter.** Not a cleaning season here, but it is when freeze and thaw does its work on unsealed concrete, and it is the argument for having sealed it in spring.

## Common questions

**Can I just do it myself with a pressure washer?**
On concrete, yes, with care. On siding, this is where most DIY damage happens, because the instinct is to use more pressure when something is not coming off, and on siding more pressure is exactly wrong. If the house is dirty rather than growing something, a garden hose and a soft brush achieve more than people expect.

**Does a house wash include the roof?**
Usually not. They are different jobs on different schedules, and the roof is every two to three years rather than annually. They do pair well in one visit if both are due.

**Will washing damage my paint?**
A soft wash should not. High pressure absolutely can, particularly on older or already-failing paint.

**What about the gutters' exterior?**
The dark vertical streaking on the outside of gutters is a specific deposit that does not simply rinse off. It is usually handled as part of a house wash rather than as part of clearing the channels, so ask whether it is included.

**I am selling. Is this worth doing?**
Yes, and it is one of the better-value items on the pre-listing list. Exterior photographs are what buyers see first, and a clean house, clean driveway and streak-free roof change the impression considerably for a small outlay.

## A sensible schedule for most homes

- **Spring:** house wash, gutters cleared after winter, windows
- **Autumn:** gutters again, before the first hard freeze
- **Every two to three years:** roof soft wash
- **Every few years:** concrete sealing, depending on the product and the traffic

That covers the majority of Treasure Valley properties. Adjust upward for shade, water, sprinklers and farmland, and downward for an open, sunny lot.

## The short version

Once a year in spring is right for most homes here. Go to twice if you have real tree cover, sit near water, or have sprinklers hitting the building. Stretch it if you are on an open lot with full sun.

The north elevation is your indicator. Check that, not the front.
