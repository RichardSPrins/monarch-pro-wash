A new build looks finished long before it is actually clean.

The trades pull out, the interior gets its final clean, and the inside looks immaculate. Then someone walks up the driveway and sees concrete dust on the flatwork, drywall haze on the windows, mortar smears on the brick and paint flecks across the siding.

That is the first impression of an expensive project, and it is the cheapest part of the whole job to get right.

## What is actually on a new build

The residue from construction is not ordinary dirt, which is why ordinary cleaning does not shift it.

**Mortar and grout smears.** Cementitious residue bonds chemically to brick, stone and concrete. It does not dissolve in water and it does not come off with pressure alone. It needs a specific treatment, correctly diluted for the substrate, or you will etch what is underneath.

**Stucco and render splatter.** Same problem, usually over a wider area and often onto surfaces nobody protected.

**Paint overspray.** Fine mist from spraying carries further than people expect. It lands on glass, hardware, trim and whatever was parked nearby, and it needs mechanical removal with the right tools rather than solvent that will damage frames.

**Drywall and silica dust.** Extremely fine, it gets everywhere, and it turns to a stubborn film the moment it is wet. Hosing it off a window without treating it first just spreads it.

**Adhesive residue.** Labels and protective film left on glass and fixtures, particularly if they have been baking in the sun for a few weeks, leave adhesive that needs solvent work.

**Caulk and sealant smears.** Silicone on glass is its own small nightmare and needs careful mechanical removal.

**Tyre marks and oil.** From deliveries and site traffic across the new flatwork.

**And then high-desert dust on top of all of it.** Every Treasure Valley site is exposed, and much of the current building is on open ground at the edge of town, out toward South Meridian, South Nampa, the Caldwell subdivisions and the foothills above Eagle. Wind puts a layer over everything, and strong UV bakes it on.

## What the service covers

A complete exterior cleanup normally includes:

**Hard surfaces washed down.** Siding, soffits, fascia, and any masonry, with the method matched to the material.

**Flatwork cleaned.** Driveway, paths, patio and garage apron, which means treating mortar residue and tyre marks, not just rinsing.

**Glass detailed.** Overspray, adhesive, labels and construction film removed, plus frames and tracks cleared of the grit that accumulates during a build.

**Debris cleared.** Offcuts, packaging, fixings and the general scatter that ends up in beds and along fence lines.

**Gutters checked and flushed.** Roofing work puts a surprising amount into a new gutter system before the house has seen a single storm.

**Fixtures and hardware wiped down.** Light fittings, house numbers, handrails and meter boxes.

What it does not include: interior cleaning, landscaping, or repairs. Those are separate trades.

## Why the method matters more here than anywhere else

Every surface on a new build is new, which means every mistake is a mistake on something that has never been used.

Fresh concrete has not fully cured and will not tolerate aggressive pressure. New stucco marks easily. Factory finishes on siding, window frames and garage doors are exactly the coatings that high pressure strips. Newly installed glass is the most expensive thing to scratch.

So the right approach is real pressure where a hard surface genuinely needs it, and a gentle soft wash on anything delicate or newly finished. A company running one setting over the whole envelope will leave marks on something that was installed last week.

Sequence matters too. The work goes from the roofline down, so that dust rinsed off a higher surface does not settle onto something already cleaned. Doing it in the wrong order means doing parts of it twice.

## When to schedule it

After every trade is finished and before the final walkthrough, handover or photography. That window is usually narrower than anyone plans for.

The common mistake is booking it too early. If exterior cleanup happens before the last trade has been back to finish a punch item, you pay for it twice. One more person on site with a caulk gun or a paint pot undoes a surprising amount.

The second mistake is booking it too late. Realtors need photographs, buyers need a walkthrough, and both tend to be scheduled before anyone thinks about the driveway.

For a listing, exterior cleanup should be done before photography, not after. The photographs are what most buyers see first, and they last far longer than the open house.

## Who pays for it

It varies, and it is worth settling in writing early.

On a custom build, it is often the general contractor's responsibility as part of final handover, though it is sometimes excluded and left with the owner. Read the contract rather than assuming.

On a spec build, the builder usually handles it, because they are selling the result.

On a remodel, it is frequently nobody's line item, which is exactly why it is so often skipped. A remodel generates the same mortar dust and overspray as a new build and rarely has a cleanup allowance attached.

For owners taking possession, it is worth asking directly whether exterior cleanup is included before closing. It is much easier to negotiate beforehand than to chase afterward.

## Residential and commercial are different jobs

For a house, the scope is the building envelope and the flatwork, and the deadline is usually a walkthrough.

For commercial, there is more of it and the constraints are different. Larger glazed areas, more hardstanding, parking lots with striping that may need protecting, and loading areas that have taken heavy traffic. Access is often restricted to outside business hours, and there is usually a tenant or a certificate-of-occupancy deadline driving the schedule.

Industrial pads, like the newer buildings out at Sky Ranch and the North Ranch business parks in Caldwell, or the commercial frontages along the Nampa corridors, generally need the same treatment at a larger scale.

## What to ask for in a quote

- Which surfaces are included, specifically
- Whether mortar and overspray removal is included or extra
- What method will be used on new stucco, new concrete and factory finishes
- Whether glass is included inside, outside, or both
- Whether gutters are included
- What happens if a trade returns and remarks something
- Proof of insurance, which matters more on an active site than almost anywhere else

That last point is not a formality. A site with other contractors still moving through it is exactly where liability questions arise.

## A realistic sequence for a handover

For anyone coordinating this, the order that avoids paying twice:

1. **All trades complete, including punch-list returns.** This is the step that slips. One more visit with a paint pot undoes the exterior clean.
2. **Interior final clean.** Doing interiors after exteriors means tracking dust back out.
3. **Exterior cleanup.** Roofline down to the driveway.
4. **Landscaping and final grading.** Anything involving soil after the hard surfaces are clean means a light re-rinse, so some coordinators prefer landscaping before step three and accept a touch-up.
5. **Photography.**
6. **Walkthrough or handover.**

The two orderings that cause the most rework are photography before cleanup and landscaping after it.

## What goes wrong when it is skipped

It rarely stays skipped. It just gets dealt with later, by someone else, at greater expense.

**Mortar residue hardens.** Left for months it becomes substantially harder to remove, and the treatment required becomes more aggressive, on brick that is now someone's house.

**Overspray cures.** Fresh overspray on glass comes off reasonably easily. After a summer of UV it is considerably more work, and the risk of scratching the glass during removal goes up.

**Construction dust gets rained into the surface.** Fine silica washed into new concrete does not simply rinse away later.

**The buyer notices.** This is the real cost. A punch list that includes "clean the exterior" at the end of a project signals a lack of care about everything that preceded it.

## If you are a builder or a remodeler

A few things worth knowing from the cleaning side.

Bundling helps. If you are putting up several homes in the same subdivision, scheduling them together is substantially cheaper per property than booking each one separately, because the setup and the travel are shared. Most of the current activity is concentrated, so this is usually practical.

Timing can be reserved. The crunch always arrives at the same point in a project, and the exterior clean tends to be the thing everyone remembers last. Booking a provisional slot against your expected completion date, and moving it as the date moves, costs nothing and avoids a scramble.

Protecting surfaces during the build is cheaper than cleaning them afterward. Obvious, routinely skipped, and the single biggest lever on what the final clean costs.

## Common questions

**How long does it take?**
A typical new home is most of a day. Larger properties and commercial buildings take longer, and access restrictions can stretch the schedule across more than one visit.

**Do you need water and power on site?**
Water, ideally. If the supply is not connected yet, say so when booking, because it changes the equipment needed.

**Can you work around other trades?**
Yes, though it is rarely the best value. Anything cleaned while another trade is still working is likely to need attention again.

**Is this different from a regular house wash?**
Substantially. A house wash deals with accumulated dirt and organic growth. Post-construction deals with bonded cementitious residue, overspray and adhesive, which are different problems needing different treatments.

**Do you do the windows inside as well?**
Exterior glass is standard. Interiors can be included, and on a new build it is usually worth it, because drywall haze does not care which side of the glass it lands on.

## Why it is worth doing properly

The whole argument is proportion. Exterior cleanup is a small fraction of the cost of any build or remodel, and it is the part that determines what the finished project actually looks like to the first person who sees it.

A buyer walking up a driveway with mortar dust on it and haze on the windows does not think "that will clean up." They think the work was rushed. Fair or not, it colours everything they look at afterward.
