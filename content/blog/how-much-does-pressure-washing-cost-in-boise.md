If you have started calling around for pressure washing in Boise, you have probably noticed the quotes are all over the place. One company gives you a number over the phone in thirty seconds. Another wants to see photos. A third says they need to walk the property first.

That spread is not random. It tells you something about how each company works, and learning to read it is the fastest way to avoid both overpaying and hiring the wrong person.

Here is what actually moves the number, what a real quote should contain, and what to do with a price that looks too good to be true.

## Why nobody can give you a real price over the phone

Pressure washing is priced by the job, not by the hour, and the job depends on things that cannot be guessed from a street address.

Two driveways of identical size can take wildly different amounts of time and product. One is five years old with a little dust on it. The other is twenty years old, has an oil stain under where a truck parked for a decade, sits under a tree that drops berries every August, and has rust bleeding out from an iron sprinkler line.

Anyone who quotes the second driveway at the first driveway's price is either going to do a fast, shallow job, or they are going to call you from the site and tell you the price has changed.

## The six things that actually drive the price

### 1. Square footage, but less than you think

Size matters, but it is rarely the biggest factor. Doubling the size of a driveway does not double the time, because setup, pre-treatment and packing up stay roughly the same. A very small job can cost more per square foot than a large one for exactly that reason.

### 2. What is actually on the surface

This is usually the real driver. Plain dust and dirt rinse off quickly. The following do not:

- Oil and transmission fluid, which need a degreaser and dwell time
- Rust and mineral bleed from iron-rich irrigation, which needs a specific treatment, not more pressure
- Organic growth like algae and moss, which needs a solution that kills it rather than a rinse that spreads it
- Paint, mortar, or construction residue, which is a different job entirely

A driveway with three of those going on is not the same job as a dusty one, no matter what the tape measure says.

### 3. The material you are cleaning

Broom-finished concrete takes pressure well. Stamped and decorative concrete does not, because aggressive cleaning strips the colour and the sealer. Pavers have joint sand that can be blown out in seconds and costs real money to replace. Older concrete from the 1940s and 1950s, which is a lot of the Boise Bench, has already been through decades of freeze and thaw and will etch if you hit it hard.

The more care a surface needs, the slower the job, and slow is what you are paying for.

### 4. Access and water

Can the truck park near the work? Is there a working outdoor spigot with decent pressure? Is the back patio reachable without carrying equipment through a gate, up steps, and around a pool? Every obstacle adds time.

### 5. What happens to the runoff

Doing this properly means protecting plants before you start, rinsing them afterward, and making sure the water and whatever came off the surface goes somewhere sensible. That takes time, and it is one of the first things cut by a company competing purely on price.

### 6. Whether you want it to stay clean

A wash makes the surface clean today. Sealing it afterward is what keeps it that way through a Treasure Valley winter. Sealing is a separate line item, and it is worth asking about up front, because sealing a surface you have just cleaned is far cheaper than cleaning it again next year.

## What a proper quote should tell you

A quote that is only a number is not really a quote. Before you accept one, you should be able to answer all of these:

- Exactly which surfaces are included, and which are not
- What method is being used on each one, and why
- Whether pre-treatment for stains is included or extra
- What happens if a stain does not fully lift
- Whether sealing is included, offered, or not available
- Who is actually turning up, and whether they are insured

If a company cannot answer those, the number is a guess, and a guess has to be revised at some point. Usually while they are standing on your driveway.

## Reading a price that looks too low

A cheap quote is not automatically a bad one. Sometimes a small operator has low overheads and does good work. But there are a few specific reasons a number comes in well under everyone else, and most of them cost you later.

**They are quoting a rinse, not a clean.** Surface dirt comes off with water. Stains do not. If the quote has no pre-treatment in it, you are paying for a rinse, and in three weeks the driveway will look much as it did before.

**They are not insured.** Insurance is a real cost, and skipping it is an easy way to undercut everyone. It is also the single biggest risk you take on. If an uninsured worker damages your siding, breaks a window, or gets hurt on your property, that becomes your problem. Ask for proof, and expect to be shown it without any fuss.

**They are using one setting on everything.** Running maximum pressure across every surface is fast, and fast is cheap. It is also how concrete gets etched, how paint comes off siding, how paver sand ends up in the flower bed, and how shingle granules end up in the gutter. The cleaning is cheap. Fixing what it did is not.

**The price changes on the day.** A number given sight unseen, with no photos and no questions, is a conversation starter rather than a quote.

## Three Boise properties, three very different jobs

It helps to see how the factors stack up in practice.

**A five-year-old driveway in a South Boise subdivision.** Broom-finished concrete, no trees overhead, used by two cars. The problem is high-desert dust and a bit of tyre marking. This is close to a best case: one surface, good access, nothing that needs specialist treatment. Sealing is worth discussing, because the slab is young enough that protecting it now genuinely pays off.

**A 1950s driveway and front walk on the Bench.** Seventy winters of freeze and thaw, an oil patch under the carport, and rust bleed from an old iron sprinkler line. The square footage might be identical to the first example, but there are now three separate treatments involved, and the concrete itself needs a careful touch because aggressive cleaning will etch surface that is already stressed. This takes longer and costs more, and anyone quoting it the same as the first driveway has not looked at it.

**A storefront frontage downtown.** Smaller area than either house, but it has gum, foot-traffic grime, limited access, and it probably has to be done before opening or after closing. Commercial work is often priced differently for exactly that reason: the constraint is not the surface, it is the schedule.

## How often is this worth doing?

Most Treasure Valley homes land somewhere around once a year for a house wash and the flatwork, which usually means spring, before the dust season gets going properly.

A few things change that:

- **Heavy tree cover** means more organic debris, more staining, and often a second visit
- **Sprinklers hitting hard surfaces** produce mineral staining fast, and that builds year on year if it is never treated
- **Sealed concrete** stays clean noticeably longer, which is the main argument for sealing in the first place
- **Commercial frontages** are usually quarterly or monthly, because the standard is different when customers are walking past

The thing nobody tells you is that regular cleaning is cheaper per visit than occasional cleaning. A surface that gets washed yearly never develops the deep staining that takes treatment and dwell time to shift. Leaving it five years does not save you money, it just moves the cost.

## Residential and commercial are priced differently

For homes, the work is priced by the job and done during normal hours.

For commercial property, two other things matter. The first is access and timing, because cleaning a storefront usually means early mornings, evenings or weekends. The second is that commercial buyers generally want a recurring schedule rather than a one-off, and a schedule is priced differently from a single visit. If you manage a property, it is usually worth asking about a maintenance arrangement rather than calling each time it looks bad.

## Questions worth asking any company you call

- Are you insured, and can I see proof?
- Who will be doing the work? Is it you, an employee, or a subcontractor?
- What is your plan if a stain does not come out?
- How do you protect landscaping?
- Do you adjust pressure for different surfaces, and how?
- Is sealing something you offer, and should I be considering it here?

The answers tell you more than the number does.

## The short version

The price of pressure washing in Boise comes down to what is on the surface, what the surface is made of, how hard it is to get to, and how carefully the work is done. Size matters least of all the factors people assume it matters most.

Get a real quote from someone who has looked at your property, ask what is included, and treat a number that arrives with no questions attached as exactly that: a number with no questions attached.
