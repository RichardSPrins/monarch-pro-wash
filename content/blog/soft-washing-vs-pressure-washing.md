Most people call it all pressure washing. That is fine in conversation, but it hides a distinction that decides whether your house comes out clean or comes out damaged.

Pressure washing and soft washing are different tools for different problems. Using the wrong one is the single most common and most expensive mistake in this trade, and the damage usually shows up months later, long after the person who caused it has moved on.

Here is how to tell which one your property needs.

## The actual difference

**Pressure washing** uses water at high force to physically blast dirt off a surface. The cleaning power comes from the pressure itself.

**Soft washing** uses water at roughly garden-hose pressure, combined with a cleaning solution that does the work chemically. The solution breaks down and kills what is growing, and the low-pressure rinse carries it away.

That is the whole distinction. One attacks the surface with force. The other treats what is on the surface and then rinses gently.

## Why it matters so much here

If the problem is dirt, force works. If the problem is alive, force does not.

Algae, mould, mildew, moss and lichen are organisms with roots. Blasting them removes what you can see and leaves the root structure behind in the pores of the material. It looks clean for a few weeks and then comes back, often faster than before, because the surface has been roughened and now holds moisture better.

A soft wash kills it at the source. That is why a properly soft-washed roof or north-facing wall stays clean for seasons rather than weeks.

## What belongs on each

### Soft wash these, always

- **Asphalt shingle roofs.** High pressure strips the mineral granules that protect the shingle from UV. Those granules are the roof. Losing them takes years off its life, and no roofer will warranty a roof that has been pressure washed.
- **Siding of any kind.** Vinyl, wood, stucco, fibre cement, painted anything. Pressure forces water up behind the panels and into the wall cavity, where it has no way out.
- **Windows and screens.** Seals fail, screens tear.
- **Fences and decks, mostly.** Softwood in particular gouges and furs up under pressure, and once the grain is raised it never looks right again.
- **Anything painted, stained or sealed.** If a coating is meant to stay on, do not aim a pressure washer at it.
- **Older masonry and historic brick.** Soft mortar will wash straight out of the joints.

### Pressure wash these

- **Driveways, patios and walkways in sound condition.** Broom-finished concrete takes pressure well and genuinely needs it to lift ingrained grime.
- **Garage floors and aprons.**
- **Sidewalks, curb strips and hardstanding.**
- **Retaining walls and block, with care.**

### It depends

- **Pavers.** They need real cleaning, but aggressive pressure blows out the joint sand that holds the whole surface together. Re-sanding afterward is normal and should be in the quote.
- **Stamped or decorative concrete.** Treat it like a coated surface, because that is what it is.
- **Older concrete.** A lot of Boise Bench flatwork dates to the 1940s and 1950s and has already been through seventy winters of freeze and thaw. It needs a measured approach, not a turbo nozzle.

## How to work out what you need

Look at what is actually on the surface.

**Is it green, black, or growing?** Algae streaks on a roof, green film on a north-facing wall, dark patches along a shaded fence line. That is organic, and it needs a soft wash. Pressure will move it and it will return.

**Is it grey, dull and uniform?** That is usually accumulated dust and grime. On concrete, pressure will handle it.

**Is it a specific mark?** Oil, rust, paint, mortar. That is a treatment problem, not a pressure problem. The right product dissolves it. More pressure just drives it deeper.

**Is the surface delicate or coated?** Then the answer is soft wash, whatever the stain is.

## What the Treasure Valley throws at you specifically

Our conditions produce a particular mix, and it is not the same mix as the Pacific Northwest or the South.

**High-desert dust.** Fine grit settles into everything all summer. It is not organic, so it rinses, but it works deep into porous surfaces and needs real cleaning on concrete.

**Hard water.** Mineral-heavy irrigation dries on glass, concrete and siding and leaves chalky white or rust-coloured staining. This is a treatment problem. No amount of pressure removes a mineral deposit; it has to be dissolved.

**Shade pockets.** We are semi-arid, so widespread roof moss is less common here than people expect. But genuinely shaded spots behave differently. Under the mature trees of the North End and Hyde Park, along the Greenbelt where humidity sits, on north-facing slopes that never dry out, conditions favour algae and it does take hold. Those roofs need a soft wash.

**Freeze and thaw.** Every winter, water that worked into concrete during autumn freezes, expands and pushes the surface apart. This is why sealing flatwork matters here, and why cleaning a slab hard enough to damage it is a genuinely expensive mistake.

## The damage, and what it costs

Worth being specific, because the consequences are not abstract:

- **Stripped roof granules.** Shortens roof life by years. A roof replacement is the most expensive thing on this list by a wide margin.
- **Water behind siding.** Leads to rot and mould inside the wall. Invisible until it is serious.
- **Etched concrete.** Permanent. The surface is physically cut, and the marks cannot be polished out.
- **Blown-out paver sand.** The pavers start shifting. Re-sanding is routine; re-laying a patio is not.
- **Stripped paint and sealer.** Repainting.
- **Failed window seals.** Replacement glazing.

Every one of those costs more than the cleaning did.

## What is actually in a soft wash solution

People hear "chemicals" and picture something harsh. In practice a soft wash mix is mostly water, with a cleaning agent that breaks down organic growth and a surfactant that helps it cling to vertical surfaces long enough to work.

The important part is dwell time. The solution needs several minutes on the surface to kill the growth properly. That is the single biggest difference between a soft wash that lasts and one that does not: a company in a hurry sprays and rinses immediately, which removes the colour but leaves the organism.

Landscaping is the other half of doing it properly. Plants get wet down before anything is applied, because saturated foliage absorbs far less, and they get rinsed again afterward. If someone starts spraying without touching your plants first, that is worth asking about.

## How long should results last?

This depends almost entirely on whether the growth was killed or just removed.

A rinse buys you a few weeks to a couple of months. The surface looks better, then the algae returns from the roots left in the pores, often looking worse because the material is now slightly rougher.

A proper soft wash on a roof or north-facing wall generally holds for a couple of years in our climate, sometimes longer on surfaces that get decent sun. Shaded, damp spots will always come back sooner than open ones, which is worth knowing before you are disappointed.

Pressure-washed concrete is different, because what returns is dust rather than growth. That is a yearly rhythm for most properties, and sealing stretches it out considerably.

## Can you do this yourself?

Honestly, some of it.

A consumer pressure washer on a sound concrete driveway, with patience and an even technique, will get you a reasonable result. The two things that catch people out are leaving wand marks, which happens when passes are uneven, and not realising how much damage the narrow nozzle can do in a single second of inattention.

Soft washing a roof is different, and this is the one to leave alone. It involves working at height, handling solution safely, protecting everything underneath, and knowing what concentration a particular roof can take. The failure modes are a fall, a dead flowerbed, or a stripped roof. None of those are worth the saving.

The in-between case is siding. It is doable at low pressure, and the main risk is forcing water behind panels or into window seals without realising it.

## Common questions

**Will soft washing kill my plants?**
Not if it is done properly. Plants get pre-wet and rinsed, and the solution is diluted. Damage happens when someone skips those steps.

**Will it damage my roof?**
Soft washing is the method roofing manufacturers specify. Pressure washing is the one that voids warranties.

**Can you pressure wash a roof if you are careful?**
No. Careful high pressure still removes granules. The granules are what protect the shingle from UV, and once they are in the gutter they are not going back.

**My neighbour's house was cleaned and it looks green again already.**
That is the classic sign of a rinse rather than a treatment. The growth was removed from the surface and left in the pores.

**Does a soft wash remove hard-water stains?**
Not on its own. Mineral staining is a different problem and needs its own treatment, which is why a quote should say which stains are being addressed and how.

## So what should you book?

For most homes, the honest answer is both, on different parts of the property, on the same visit.

A typical house needs a soft wash on the roof and siding and a pressure wash on the driveway, patio and walkways. That is not an upsell, it is just what the surfaces require. Any company that turns up and uses one setting on all of it is making their day simpler at your expense.

What you actually want to hear from whoever you hire is which method they plan to use on which surface, and why. If they have an answer, they have thought about your property. If they do not, they have one setting.
