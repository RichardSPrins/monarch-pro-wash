How Much Does Pressure Washing Cost in Boise?

What actually drives the price of pressure washing in Boise and the Treasure Valley, how to compare quotes properly, and the warning signs in a number that looks too good.

Richard Prins · 2026-10-06 · 8 min read · 1711 words · /blog/how-much-does-pressure-washing-cost-in-boise

If you have started calling around for pressure washing in Boise, you have probably noticed the quotes are all over the place. One company gives you a number over the phone in thirty seconds. Another wants to see photos. A third says they need to walk the property first.

That spread is not random. It tells you something about how each company works, and learning to read it is the fastest way to avoid both overpaying and hiring the wrong person.

Here is what actually moves the number, what a real quote should contain, and what to do with a price that looks too good to be true.

Why nobody can give you a real price over the phone

Pressure washing is priced by the job, not by the hour, and the job depends on things that cannot be guessed from a street address.

Two driveways of identical size can take wildly different amounts of time and product. One is five years old with a little dust on it. The other is twenty years old, has an oil stain under where a truck parked for a decade, sits under a tree that drops berries every August, and has rust bleeding out from an iron sprinkler line.

Anyone who quotes the second driveway at the first driveway's price is either going to do a fast, shallow job, or they are going to call you from the site and tell you the price has changed.

The six things that actually drive the price

1. Square footage, but less than you think

Size matters, but it is rarely the biggest factor. Doubling the size of a driveway does not double the time, because setup, pre-treatment and packing up stay roughly the same. A very small job can cost more per square foot than a large one for exactly that reason.

2. What is actually on the surface

This is usually the real driver. Plain dust and dirt rinse off quickly. The following do not:

  • Oil and transmission fluid, which need a degreaser and dwell time
  • Rust and mineral bleed from iron-rich irrigation, which needs a specific treatment, not more pressure
  • Organic growth like algae and moss, which needs a solution that kills it rather than a rinse that spreads it
  • Paint, mortar, or construction residue, which is a different job entirely

A driveway with three of those going on is not the same job as a dusty one, no matter what the tape measure says.

3. The material you are cleaning

Broom-finished concrete takes pressure well. Stamped and decorative concrete does not, because aggressive cleaning strips the colour and the sealer. Pavers have joint sand that can be blown out in seconds and costs real money to replace. Older concrete from the 1940s and 1950s, which is a lot of the Boise Bench, has already been through decades of freeze and thaw and will etch if you hit it hard.

The more care a surface needs, the slower the job, and slow is what you are paying for.

4. Access and water

Can the truck park near the work? Is there a working outdoor spigot with decent pressure? Is the back patio reachable without carrying equipment through a gate, up steps, and around a pool? Every obstacle adds time.

5. What happens to the runoff

Doing this properly means protecting plants before you start, rinsing them afterward, and making sure the water and whatever came off the surface goes somewhere sensible. That takes time, and it is one of the first things cut by a company competing purely on price.

6. Whether you want it to stay clean

A wash makes the surface clean today. Sealing it afterward is what keeps it that way through a Treasure Valley winter. Sealing is a separate line item, and it is worth asking about up front, because sealing a surface you have just cleaned is far cheaper than cleaning it again next year.

What a proper quote should tell you

A quote that is only a number is not really a quote. Before you accept one, you should be able to answer all of these:

  • Exactly which surfaces are included, and which are not
  • What method is being used on each one, and why
  • Whether pre-treatment for stains is included or extra
  • What happens if a stain does not fully lift
  • Whether sealing is included, offered, or not available
  • Who is actually turning up, and whether they are insured

If a company cannot answer those, the number is a guess, and a guess has to be revised at some point. Usually while they are standing on your driveway.

Reading a price that looks too low

A cheap quote is not automatically a bad one. Sometimes a small operator has low overheads and does good work. But there are a few specific reasons a number comes in well under everyone else, and most of them cost you later.

They are quoting a rinse, not a clean. Surface dirt comes off with water. Stains do not. If the quote has no pre-treatment in it, you are paying for a rinse, and in three weeks the driveway will look much as it did before.

They are not insured. Insurance is a real cost, and skipping it is an easy way to undercut everyone. It is also the single biggest risk you take on. If an uninsured worker damages your siding, breaks a window, or gets hurt on your property, that becomes your problem. Ask for proof, and expect to be shown it without any fuss.

They are using one setting on everything. Running maximum pressure across every surface is fast, and fast is cheap. It is also how concrete gets etched, how paint comes off siding, how paver sand ends up in the flower bed, and how shingle granules end up in the gutter. The cleaning is cheap. Fixing what it did is not.

The price changes on the day. A number given sight unseen, with no photos and no questions, is a conversation starter rather than a quote.

Three Boise properties, three very different jobs

It helps to see how the factors stack up in practice.

A five-year-old driveway in a South Boise subdivision. Broom-finished concrete, no trees overhead, used by two cars. The problem is high-desert dust and a bit of tyre marking. This is close to a best case: one surface, good access, nothing that needs specialist treatment. Sealing is worth discussing, because the slab is young enough that protecting it now genuinely pays off.

A 1950s driveway and front walk on the Bench. Seventy winters of freeze and thaw, an oil patch under the carport, and rust bleed from an old iron sprinkler line. The square footage might be identical to the first example, but there are now three separate treatments involved, and the concrete itself needs a careful touch because aggressive cleaning will etch surface that is already stressed. This takes longer and costs more, and anyone quoting it the same as the first driveway has not looked at it.

A storefront frontage downtown. Smaller area than either house, but it has gum, foot-traffic grime, limited access, and it probably has to be done before opening or after closing. Commercial work is often priced differently for exactly that reason: the constraint is not the surface, it is the schedule.

How often is this worth doing?

Most Treasure Valley homes land somewhere around once a year for a house wash and the flatwork, which usually means spring, before the dust season gets going properly.

A few things change that:

  • Heavy tree cover means more organic debris, more staining, and often a second visit
  • Sprinklers hitting hard surfaces produce mineral staining fast, and that builds year on year if it is never treated
  • Sealed concrete stays clean noticeably longer, which is the main argument for sealing in the first place
  • Commercial frontages are usually quarterly or monthly, because the standard is different when customers are walking past

The thing nobody tells you is that regular cleaning is cheaper per visit than occasional cleaning. A surface that gets washed yearly never develops the deep staining that takes treatment and dwell time to shift. Leaving it five years does not save you money, it just moves the cost.

Residential and commercial are priced differently

For homes, the work is priced by the job and done during normal hours.

For commercial property, two other things matter. The first is access and timing, because cleaning a storefront usually means early mornings, evenings or weekends. The second is that commercial buyers generally want a recurring schedule rather than a one-off, and a schedule is priced differently from a single visit. If you manage a property, it is usually worth asking about a maintenance arrangement rather than calling each time it looks bad.

The short version

The price of pressure washing in Boise comes down to what is on the surface, what the surface is made of, how hard it is to get to, and how carefully the work is done. Size matters least of all the factors people assume it matters most.

Do I Need Soft Washing or Pressure Washing?

The difference matters more than most people realise, and picking wrong is how roofs lose granules and siding gets gouged. A plain guide to which method belongs on which surface.

Richard Prins · 2026-10-02 · 7 min read · 1668 words · /blog/soft-washing-vs-pressure-washing

Most people call it all pressure washing. That is fine in conversation, but it hides a distinction that decides whether your house comes out clean or comes out damaged.

Pressure washing and soft washing are different tools for different problems. Using the wrong one is the single most common and most expensive mistake in this trade, and the damage usually shows up months later, long after the person who caused it has moved on.

Here is how to tell which one your property needs.

The actual difference

Pressure washing uses water at high force to physically blast dirt off a surface. The cleaning power comes from the pressure itself.

Soft washing uses water at roughly garden-hose pressure, combined with a cleaning solution that does the work chemically. The solution breaks down and kills what is growing, and the low-pressure rinse carries it away.

That is the whole distinction. One attacks the surface with force. The other treats what is on the surface and then rinses gently.

Why it matters so much here

If the problem is dirt, force works. If the problem is alive, force does not.

Algae, mould, mildew, moss and lichen are organisms with roots. Blasting them removes what you can see and leaves the root structure behind in the pores of the material. It looks clean for a few weeks and then comes back, often faster than before, because the surface has been roughened and now holds moisture better.

A soft wash kills it at the source. That is why a properly soft-washed roof or north-facing wall stays clean for seasons rather than weeks.

What belongs on each

Soft wash these, always

  • Asphalt shingle roofs. High pressure strips the mineral granules that protect the shingle from UV. Those granules are the roof. Losing them takes years off its life, and no roofer will warranty a roof that has been pressure washed.
  • Siding of any kind. Vinyl, wood, stucco, fibre cement, painted anything. Pressure forces water up behind the panels and into the wall cavity, where it has no way out.
  • Windows and screens. Seals fail, screens tear.
  • Fences and decks, mostly. Softwood in particular gouges and furs up under pressure, and once the grain is raised it never looks right again.
  • Anything painted, stained or sealed. If a coating is meant to stay on, do not aim a pressure washer at it.
  • Older masonry and historic brick. Soft mortar will wash straight out of the joints.

Pressure wash these

  • Driveways, patios and walkways in sound condition. Broom-finished concrete takes pressure well and genuinely needs it to lift ingrained grime.
  • Garage floors and aprons.
  • Sidewalks, curb strips and hardstanding.
  • Retaining walls and block, with care.

It depends

  • Pavers. They need real cleaning, but aggressive pressure blows out the joint sand that holds the whole surface together. Re-sanding afterward is normal and should be in the quote.
  • Stamped or decorative concrete. Treat it like a coated surface, because that is what it is.
  • Older concrete. A lot of Boise Bench flatwork dates to the 1940s and 1950s and has already been through seventy winters of freeze and thaw. It needs a measured approach, not a turbo nozzle.

How to work out what you need

Look at what is actually on the surface.

Is it green, black, or growing? Algae streaks on a roof, green film on a north-facing wall, dark patches along a shaded fence line. That is organic, and it needs a soft wash. Pressure will move it and it will return.

Is it grey, dull and uniform? That is usually accumulated dust and grime. On concrete, pressure will handle it.

Is it a specific mark? Oil, rust, paint, mortar. That is a treatment problem, not a pressure problem. The right product dissolves it. More pressure just drives it deeper.

Is the surface delicate or coated? Then the answer is soft wash, whatever the stain is.

What the Treasure Valley throws at you specifically

Our conditions produce a particular mix, and it is not the same mix as the Pacific Northwest or the South.

High-desert dust. Fine grit settles into everything all summer. It is not organic, so it rinses, but it works deep into porous surfaces and needs real cleaning on concrete.

Hard water. Mineral-heavy irrigation dries on glass, concrete and siding and leaves chalky white or rust-coloured staining. This is a treatment problem. No amount of pressure removes a mineral deposit; it has to be dissolved.

Shade pockets. We are semi-arid, so widespread roof moss is less common here than people expect. But genuinely shaded spots behave differently. Under the mature trees of the North End and Hyde Park, along the Greenbelt where humidity sits, on north-facing slopes that never dry out, conditions favour algae and it does take hold. Those roofs need a soft wash.

Freeze and thaw. Every winter, water that worked into concrete during autumn freezes, expands and pushes the surface apart. This is why sealing flatwork matters here, and why cleaning a slab hard enough to damage it is a genuinely expensive mistake.

The damage, and what it costs

Worth being specific, because the consequences are not abstract:

  • Stripped roof granules. Shortens roof life by years. A roof replacement is the most expensive thing on this list by a wide margin.
  • Water behind siding. Leads to rot and mould inside the wall. Invisible until it is serious.
  • Etched concrete. Permanent. The surface is physically cut, and the marks cannot be polished out.
  • Blown-out paver sand. The pavers start shifting. Re-sanding is routine; re-laying a patio is not.
  • Stripped paint and sealer. Repainting.
  • Failed window seals. Replacement glazing.

Every one of those costs more than the cleaning did.

What is actually in a soft wash solution

People hear "chemicals" and picture something harsh. In practice a soft wash mix is mostly water, with a cleaning agent that breaks down organic growth and a surfactant that helps it cling to vertical surfaces long enough to work.

The important part is dwell time. The solution needs several minutes on the surface to kill the growth properly. That is the single biggest difference between a soft wash that lasts and one that does not: a company in a hurry sprays and rinses immediately, which removes the colour but leaves the organism.

Landscaping is the other half of doing it properly. Plants get wet down before anything is applied, because saturated foliage absorbs far less, and they get rinsed again afterward. If someone starts spraying without touching your plants first, that is worth asking about.

How long should results last?

This depends almost entirely on whether the growth was killed or just removed.

A rinse buys you a few weeks to a couple of months. The surface looks better, then the algae returns from the roots left in the pores, often looking worse because the material is now slightly rougher.

A proper soft wash on a roof or north-facing wall generally holds for a couple of years in our climate, sometimes longer on surfaces that get decent sun. Shaded, damp spots will always come back sooner than open ones, which is worth knowing before you are disappointed.

Pressure-washed concrete is different, because what returns is dust rather than growth. That is a yearly rhythm for most properties, and sealing stretches it out considerably.

Can you do this yourself?

Honestly, some of it.

A consumer pressure washer on a sound concrete driveway, with patience and an even technique, will get you a reasonable result. The two things that catch people out are leaving wand marks, which happens when passes are uneven, and not realising how much damage the narrow nozzle can do in a single second of inattention.

Soft washing a roof is different, and this is the one to leave alone. It involves working at height, handling solution safely, protecting everything underneath, and knowing what concentration a particular roof can take. The failure modes are a fall, a dead flowerbed, or a stripped roof. None of those are worth the saving.

The in-between case is siding. It is doable at low pressure, and the main risk is forcing water behind panels or into window seals without realising it.

Common questions

Will soft washing kill my plants? Not if it is done properly. Plants get pre-wet and rinsed, and the solution is diluted. Damage happens when someone skips those steps.

Will it damage my roof? Soft washing is the method roofing manufacturers specify. Pressure washing is the one that voids warranties.

Can you pressure wash a roof if you are careful? No. Careful high pressure still removes granules. The granules are what protect the shingle from UV, and once they are in the gutter they are not going back.

My neighbour's house was cleaned and it looks green again already. That is the classic sign of a rinse rather than a treatment. The growth was removed from the surface and left in the pores.

Does a soft wash remove hard-water stains? Not on its own. Mineral staining is a different problem and needs its own treatment, which is why a quote should say which stains are being addressed and how.

So what should you book?

For most homes, the honest answer is both, on different parts of the property, on the same visit.

A typical house needs a soft wash on the roof and siding and a pressure wash on the driveway, patio and walkways. That is not an upsell, it is just what the surfaces require. Any company that turns up and uses one setting on all of it is making their day simpler at your expense.

What you actually want to hear from whoever you hire is which method they plan to use on which surface, and why. If they have an answer, they have thought about your property. If they do not, they have one setting.

How Much Does Roof Cleaning Cost in the Treasure Valley?

What drives the price of roof cleaning in Boise and the surrounding valley, why the cheapest quote is often the most expensive one, and how to tell a soft wash from a roof-damaging shortcut.

Richard Prins · 2026-09-28 · 8 min read · 1764 words · /blog/how-much-does-roof-cleaning-cost

Roof cleaning is the service where the gap between the cheapest quote and the right quote matters most. Get it wrong on a driveway and you have some marks in the concrete. Get it wrong on a roof and you have taken years off the most expensive component of your house.

So before talking about what it costs, it is worth being clear about what you are actually buying.

What you are paying for

The black streaks running down most Treasure Valley roofs are not dirt. They are Gloeocapsa magma, an algae that feeds on the limestone filler used in asphalt shingles. It spreads across the slopes that stay damp longest, which here means north-facing pitches and anything under tree cover.

Left alone, it holds moisture against the shingle. In shaded, consistently damp spots, moss follows, and moss is the genuinely destructive one: it roots at the shingle edges and lifts them, which is how water gets underneath.

Roof cleaning done properly kills that growth at the root with a low-pressure soft wash. The solution does the work. Low pressure carries it away. Nobody blasts anything.

What you are paying for is the method, the care taken around everything beneath the roof, and the fact that the roof is in the same condition afterward as it was before, only clean.

What moves the price

Roof size and pitch

Square footage matters, but pitch matters more. A steep roof is slower and more hazardous to work, which means more setup, more care and more time. A single-storey ranch with a shallow pitch is a very different job from a two-storey with steep gables, even at similar square footage.

Height and access

Can ladders be set safely? Is there a second storey, or a third? Are there sections only reachable over a conservatory or a deck? Every complication adds time.

How much growth there is

A roof with light streaking on one slope needs less solution and less dwell time than a roof that has been accumulating for ten years and has moss established along the valleys. Severity is a real cost driver, and it is the one that grows if you wait.

Roof material

Asphalt shingle is the common case here and is straightforward with the right method. Tile, metal, cedar shake and composite each behave differently and need their own approach. Premium materials need more care, not less.

What is underneath

This is the part people forget. A roof that overhangs mature planting, a pool, a vegetable garden or a newly stained deck takes longer, because all of it has to be protected before anything is applied and rinsed afterward.

Whether the gutters are done at the same time

Most of what comes off a roof ends up in the gutters. Doing both in one visit is almost always cheaper than two separate trips, and it is the single most common bundle for good reason.

Why the cheapest quote is often the most expensive

There are really only three ways to make roof cleaning dramatically cheaper than everyone else, and all three cost you later.

Pressure wash it. Fast, visually effective on day one, and it strips the mineral granules off your shingles. Those granules are what protect the asphalt from UV. Once they are in the gutter, the shingle starts aging rapidly. Most manufacturers void their warranty over it, and most roofers can spot it immediately.

Rinse instead of treat. Spray the solution and immediately wash it off. The surface looks clean and the organism is still alive in the pores. It comes back within months, usually worse, because the surface is now rougher.

Skip the protection. Not pre-wetting plants, not covering what needs covering, not rinsing afterward. Saves twenty minutes and kills a flowerbed.

If a quote is dramatically below everything else, it is worth asking which of those three is being skipped. Sometimes the honest answer is none of them and the company simply has low overheads. But it is a fair question.

Questions that separate the good from the cheap

  • Are you soft washing or pressure washing? (The only acceptable answer for a shingle roof is soft washing.)
  • What is your dwell time before rinsing?
  • How do you protect the landscaping underneath?
  • Are you insured, and can I see proof?
  • Does this include clearing the gutters, or is that separate?
  • What happens if streaking comes back in six months?

A company that cleans roofs properly will have fast, specific answers. Vagueness here is informative.

How often does a roof need this?

In our semi-arid climate, less often than in wetter parts of the country. Every two to three years covers most Treasure Valley homes.

What shortens that interval:

  • Heavy tree cover. The mature canopy in the North End, Hyde Park and the older Nampa and Caldwell districts keeps slopes damp and drops organic debris that feeds growth.
  • River and Greenbelt proximity. Homes backing the Boise River, through Harris Ranch and Barber Valley, sit in humidity that does not fully burn off.
  • North-facing pitches. Any slope that does not get direct sun stays damp longer and will always go first.

What lengthens it: open lots with full sun on all slopes, which describes a lot of newer South Meridian and South Nampa construction.

Worth saying plainly: widespread Pacific-Northwest-style roof moss is not typical here. We get around ten to twelve inches of rain a year. Where conditions genuinely favour growth is in specific shaded pockets, and anyone telling you every roof in the valley is covered in moss is selling rather than inspecting.

Roof cleaning is not something to price sight unseen, because pitch, access and severity move the number too much. Photos help a lot, and often that is enough to get close without a site visit.

What it costs to wait

The argument for doing this sooner rather than later is not aesthetic.

Algae holds moisture against the shingle. Moss lifts shingle edges and lets water underneath. A roof that should have lasted twenty-five years starts failing at eighteen. Against the cost of a roof replacement, periodic cleaning is one of the better-value maintenance items on a house, in the same category as cleaning gutters or servicing a furnace.

The streaks are the visible part. What they are doing to the shingle underneath is the actual problem.

What actually happens on the day

Knowing the sequence makes it much easier to tell whether the job you got was the job you paid for.

Inspection first. Before anything is mixed, the roof gets looked at: material, pitch, condition, where the growth actually is, and whether there are any loose or damaged shingles that need flagging. A roof clean is not a repair, but you should be told if something needs a roofer.

Protection second. Everything underneath gets handled before a drop of solution is applied. Plants are saturated with plain water first, because wet foliage absorbs far less. Anything that cannot be wet down gets covered. Downspout discharge points get considered, because that is where the runoff ends up.

Application. The solution goes on at low pressure, worked from the appropriate direction so it flows down the slope rather than being driven under the shingle edges.

Dwell. This is the step that separates a lasting clean from a cosmetic one. The solution needs time to kill the organism rather than just loosen its colour. Rushing here is the most common corner cut in the trade.

Rinse and final check. Low-pressure rinse, landscaping watered again, gutters checked for what has washed down, and a walk round so you can see the result while the person who did it is still there.

A thorough job on a typical home is a half-day. If someone is in and out in forty minutes, something in that sequence did not happen.

The economics, plainly

Put next to the thing it protects, periodic roof cleaning is not a large number.

A roof replacement is one of the biggest line items a homeowner ever faces. Algae and moss do not cause that bill on their own, but they accelerate it, and they do it quietly over years. The shingle holds moisture it was not designed to hold, the granule layer degrades faster, and the slope that always stays damp fails first.

Cleaning every two to three years in our climate sits in the same maintenance bracket as clearing gutters or servicing a furnace: unglamorous, inexpensive relative to the asset, and obviously worth it in hindsight.

There is a resale angle too. Black streaking down a roof reads as neglect from the kerb, whatever the condition of the house behind it. It is one of the cheapest things to fix before listing.

Common questions

Will the streaks come back? Eventually, yes, because the spores are airborne and the conditions that favoured growth have not changed. But a proper treatment kills what is there, so you are starting from zero rather than from roots left in the surface. In our climate that usually means a couple of years.

Is this safe for my shingles? Soft washing is the method the shingle manufacturers specify. The thing that is not safe is pressure.

What about my plants? Pre-wet, protected, and rinsed afterward. Done properly, planting is fine. Nearly all plant damage in this trade comes from skipping the pre-wet.

Can you clean the gutters at the same time? Yes, and it is worth doing. Most of what comes off a roof ends up in the gutters, and one visit costs less than two.

Do you walk on the roof? As little as possible. Soft washing is largely done from ladders and from the ground with the right equipment, which is both safer and better for the shingles.

My roof is metal, not shingle. Different approach, still low pressure. Metal has its own considerations around coatings and fixings.

The short version

Price depends on pitch, access, how much growth has built up, what the roof is made of, and what sits underneath it. Size matters less than people expect, and severity matters more.

The method is not negotiable. A shingle roof gets a soft wash. Anyone proposing pressure on it is offering to damage your roof for money, whatever the quote says.

When Should You Clean Your Gutters in Boise?

Timing matters more than frequency with gutters in the Treasure Valley. Here is when to book, why the autumn window is tighter than people think, and what a clog actually costs.

Richard Prins · 2026-09-22 · 7 min read · 1565 words · /blog/when-to-clean-gutters-boise

Gutters are the least interesting thing on your house and one of the most consequential. They have a single job, and when they stop doing it, the damage does not stay in the gutter.

The question is not really how often. It is when.

What a clogged gutter actually does

When leaves and needles pack the channel or block a downspout, rainwater and snowmelt have nowhere to go, so they go over the edge.

That overflow does three things:

It runs down your fascia. Constant water against painted wood is how rot starts. Fascia replacement is not a small job, because it usually means working along the whole roof edge.

It pools against the foundation. Gutters exist to move roof water away from the house. A blocked one concentrates it in exactly the place you least want it. Over years that means settlement, and in the short term it means a damp basement or crawlspace.

It freezes. This is the Treasure Valley specific one, and it is the reason timing matters here more than in milder places. Water sitting in a blocked gutter through a freeze-thaw cycle expands as it freezes, then thaws, then freezes again. That cycle strains the gutter brackets, backs water up under the roof edge, and works into places water should never be.

A clogged gutter is cheap to clear and expensive to ignore. That is the whole argument.

The honest answer on timing

For most Boise-area homes, twice a year, and the two windows are not equally important.

Late autumn, after the leaves are down

This is the one that matters most. The goal is to clear everything before the first hard freeze, so that whatever winter brings has a clear path out.

The timing is tighter than people expect. You want the trees to have actually finished dropping, but you want it done before the weather turns. In practice that means late October into November, and the window closes quickly. Booking in September for a November slot is sensible. Calling in late December is often too late to prevent the problem you are calling about.

Spring, after the valley has done its worst

The second visit clears what winter put in there: broken twigs, grit washed off the roof, the last of the autumn debris that was frozen in place, and the first of the seed and blossom drop.

Spring is also when it makes sense to check the whole system rather than just the channel. Are the downspouts actually flowing? Are brackets still holding after a winter of ice load? Is water discharging far enough from the foundation?

When twice a year is not enough

Some properties need more, and it is almost always about what is overhead.

Mature tree cover. The decades-old canopy in the North End and Hyde Park is beautiful and it fills gutters solid. Old Town Meridian and the streets around Kleiner Park are similar, as is the elm canopy over the Caldwell historic districts and Old Nampa. Those homes often want three visits, with an extra pass partway through the autumn drop.

Pine and conifer. Needles are worse than leaves, because they drop year-round, they knit together into a mat, and they slip straight into downspouts where leaves would bridge across.

Properties on the ag edge. Homes out toward Lake Lowell, along the farm corridors, or anywhere near open ground collect field dust and crop debris that leaves do not account for.

Anything near new construction. Jobsite dust is relentless and it settles everywhere, including in your gutters.

When once a year is genuinely fine

If you are on an open lot with no significant tree cover, which describes a lot of newer South Meridian, South Nampa and the outer subdivisions, one thorough autumn clean is often enough. There simply is not the organic load.

The thing to watch is that "no trees on my lot" is not the same as "no trees upwind."

Signs you are already overdue

  • Water spilling over the edge during rain rather than running to the downspout
  • Streaking or dark staining down the exterior of the gutter
  • Plants growing out of the gutter, which means there is enough soil in there to support them
  • Sagging anywhere along the run
  • A downspout that stays dry while the gutter is clearly full
  • Water pooling along the foundation after rain

Any one of those means it is time now, not at the next scheduled window.

What a proper gutter clean includes

This is worth knowing, because "gutter cleaning" covers a range of thoroughness.

A complete job clears the channels by hand rather than just blowing debris around, flushes every downspout until water runs clear at the bottom, checks that discharge is directed away from the foundation, and bags and removes the debris rather than leaving it in your beds.

Brightening the gutter exterior is a separate thing, and it is what removes the dark vertical streaking that makes an otherwise clean house look tired. That streaking is not dirt that rinses off; it needs treating.

Gutter guards

Worth addressing, because people ask.

Guards reduce how much gets in. They do not eliminate maintenance. Fine debris, grit, shingle granules and seed still accumulate, and on some designs the debris builds on top of the guard instead of in the gutter, which is harder to see and harder to clear.

If you have guards, you still need periodic inspection, just less frequently. Anyone telling you guards mean never thinking about gutters again is overselling.

Doing it yourself

Clearing a single-storey gutter from a stable ladder on level ground is a reasonable DIY job if you are comfortable on a ladder and have someone around.

What makes it not worth it: two-storey work, steep or uneven ground, anything requiring the ladder to lean against the gutter itself, which bends it. Ladder falls are the most common serious home-maintenance injury by a wide margin, and gutters are the most common reason people are up there.

Why Treasure Valley Hard Water Stains Your Windows, and How to Get It Off

Those cloudy spots are mineral deposits, not dirt, which is why wiping does nothing. What causes them here, how to remove them, and how to stop them coming back.

Richard Prins · 2026-09-15 · 8 min read · 1749 words · /blog/hard-water-stains-on-windows

You clean a window, it dries, and the cloudy patches are still there. You go at it harder with glass cleaner and a cloth, and they are still there.

That is not a cleaning failure. You are not dealing with dirt.

What is actually on the glass

Water in the Treasure Valley carries dissolved minerals, mostly calcium and magnesium. When a droplet lands on glass and evaporates in the sun, the water leaves and the minerals do not. What stays behind is a microscopic deposit bonded to the surface.

One droplet is invisible. Thousands of them, in the same places, every morning for a few summers, is the cloudy haze you are looking at.

This is why wiping does nothing. A cloth moves dirt that is sitting on glass. A mineral deposit is chemically attached to it. You cannot wipe it off any more than you could wipe limescale out of a kettle.

Where it comes from

Four sources, in roughly descending order of how much damage they do.

Sprinkler overspray. This is the big one and it is almost entirely avoidable. A sprinkler head that catches the bottom of a window, every morning, through an entire irrigation season, is the single most reliable way to produce severe staining. The pattern gives it away: worst at the bottom of the pane, fading upward, and concentrated on whichever elevation faces the lawn.

Rain running off untreated surfaces. Water sheeting down a wall or off a roof picks up minerals and debris along the way and carries them across the glass below.

Irrigation and well water. Properties on private wells, particularly out on acreage, often deal with harder water than those on municipal supply.

Hose washing with hard water. Washing a window and letting it air dry in direct sun deposits a fresh layer of minerals as it goes. This is why windows sometimes look worse after being cleaned.

It is not the same everywhere in the valley

Worth being accurate about this, because the generic version of this article gets it wrong.

Water hardness varies meaningfully across the Treasure Valley. Meridian and Kuna sit at the harder end, and homes there see mineral spotting faster and more severely than most. Emmett is harder still.

Nampa and Caldwell municipal water is only moderately hard. If someone is pitching you an emergency about extremely hard water on a city-served home in Nampa, they are reading from a script rather than looking at your property. You will still get spotting, particularly from sprinklers, but the severity is different.

For Star, Payette and Mountain Home, reliable published figures are harder to come by, so treat any confident number you are quoted with some scepticism. Regional water runs hard, and that is about as specific as anyone should get without testing.

Private wells are their own case regardless of location.

Why it gets worse, not just dirtier

Mineral staining is progressive in a way ordinary dirt is not.

A fresh deposit sits on the surface and comes off reasonably easily. Left in place through heat and UV, it begins to bond more tightly, and over a long enough period it starts to etch the glass itself. At that point you are no longer removing a deposit, you are dealing with physically damaged glass, and no cleaning process will fully restore it.

This is the argument for dealing with it sooner. The same stain is cheap to remove this year and may be permanent in five.

How it actually comes off

The deposit is alkaline, so it is removed with a mild acidic treatment that dissolves the bond, followed by a thorough neutralising rinse. The treatment does the work. Mechanical scrubbing alone does not, and abrasives scratch glass.

The things that matter in doing it properly:

The right strength for the severity. Light spotting needs very little. Heavy, aged staining needs more dwell time rather than more aggression.

Protecting everything around the glass. This is the part amateurs get wrong. The treatment that dissolves mineral deposits will also mark window frames, particularly anodised aluminium, and will damage plants below. Frames get protected and planting gets pre-wet and rinsed.

Neutralising fully. Residue left in a frame corner continues to work on whatever it is touching.

Pure water for the final rinse. Rinsing with the same hard water that caused the problem redeposits minerals as it dries. A proper finish uses filtered water so there is nothing left behind to dry into a spot.

What you can try yourself

For light, recent spotting, a 50/50 white vinegar and distilled water solution, left on the glass for several minutes and then squeegeed off, will often make a real difference. Distilled rather than tap water matters, for the reason above.

What not to do:

  • Abrasive pads or powders. They will scratch glass, and scratches are permanent.
  • Razor blades. Tempered glass in particular scratches easily, and much modern glazing has coatings that a blade destroys.
  • Strong acid from a hardware store. It works on the deposit and it also attacks frames, seals and whatever is planted underneath.
  • Letting it dry in the sun. Always squeegee or dry the glass rather than letting it air dry.

Heavy or long-established staining is generally past the point where a DIY approach gets a satisfying result, mostly because the chemistry needs to be stronger and that is exactly when protecting everything around the glass starts to matter.

Stopping it coming back

Removal is a one-off. Prevention is the thing that saves you money.

Adjust your sprinklers. This is the highest-value item on the list by a distance. Walk the irrigation zones, watch where the heads actually throw, and move or adjust anything hitting glass or siding. Ten minutes of adjustment prevents an entire season of staining.

Water early. Running irrigation before sunrise means any overspray is less likely to bake on in direct sun.

Clean on a schedule rather than when it looks bad. Twice a year keeps deposits from establishing. Once every few years means each clean is a restoration job.

Consider a protective coating. Glass sealants make the surface more hydrophobic so water sheets off rather than beading and sitting. They are not permanent, but they buy time and make routine cleaning easier.

Deal with the tracks too. Grit and dead insects in the track hold moisture against the frame and get dragged across the glass when the window moves.

When it is worth calling someone

Three situations where professional treatment is the sensible call: staining you have already tried to remove and could not, glass you cannot safely reach, and anything where you are worried about damaging frames or planting.

The fourth is storefront glass, where the standard is simply different. A customer forms an impression of a business from the front window before they reach the door, and spotting on a commercial frontage reads as neglect in a way it does not on a house.

Screens, tracks and frames

Glass gets the attention, but the rest of the window assembly is usually where the problem is being fed from.

Screens hold a surprising amount of dust, pollen and mineral residue. Washing the glass and putting a dirty screen back in front of it undoes most of the work the first time it rains. Screens come out, get washed separately, and go back in dry.

Tracks collect grit, dead insects and water. That slurry gets dragged across the bottom of the glass every time the window opens, and it holds moisture against the frame, which is how seals degrade early. Clearing tracks is unglamorous and makes a visible difference.

Frames vary in what they tolerate. Anodised aluminium in particular marks if mineral treatment is left on it. Painted and vinyl frames are more forgiving but still need rinsing. This is the main reason strong hardware-store acid is a bad idea on your own windows: it will deal with the deposit and quietly ruin the frame.

Interior and exterior are different problems

Exterior glass deals with minerals, dust and weather. Interior glass deals with cooking residue, hand marks, pet noses and, in newer airtight homes, a fine film that builds up from everyday indoor air.

They need different handling, and a clean that only addresses one side always disappoints, because glass is transparent and you see both at once. If you are booking a window clean and the quote does not say whether interiors are included, ask.

How often is reasonable

For most Treasure Valley homes, twice a year works: once in spring after the winter grime and once in autumn before the weather closes in.

Reasons to go more often:

  • Sprinklers hitting the glass, until the heads are adjusted
  • Living on or near the ag edge, out toward Lake Lowell or along the farm corridors, where field dust is constant
  • Proximity to active construction, which produces fine dust that sticks
  • A storefront, where the standard is a matter of how the business is perceived rather than personal preference

Commercial frontages are commonly monthly or quarterly. It is the first thing a customer sees and the cheapest impression a business can buy.

Common questions

Will the spots definitely come off? Light and moderate deposits, almost always. Severe staining that has been left for years may have begun etching the glass, in which case treatment improves it significantly but may not fully restore it. Anyone promising a perfect result on badly etched glass before seeing it is guessing.

Does rain make it worse? Rain itself is relatively clean. What causes trouble is rain running across a dirty surface above the window and carrying that down the glass.

Why do my windows look worse right after I wash them? Almost always because they air dried in the sun, which deposits a fresh layer of minerals from the wash water itself. Squeegee or dry the glass.

Is it worth sealing the glass? On problem elevations, often yes. It will not stop deposits forming, but it makes water sheet off rather than sit, which reduces how much is left behind.

The short version

Cloudy window spots are mineral deposits, not dirt, so cleaning harder will never work. They come mostly from sprinkler overspray, they get harder to remove the longer they sit, and left long enough they etch the glass permanently.

Fix the sprinklers first. That one change prevents most of it.

Should You Seal Your Concrete Driveway in Idaho?

Freeze and thaw is the thing that destroys concrete here, and sealing is the main defence. When it is worth doing, when it is too late, and what the process actually involves.

Richard Prins · 2026-09-08 · 8 min read · 1756 words · /blog/should-you-seal-concrete-driveway-idaho

Concrete looks like the most permanent thing on your property. It is not. It is porous, it absorbs water, and in a climate that swings above and below freezing dozens of times each winter, that combination is what eventually destroys it.

Sealing is the main defence. Here is when it is worth doing, when the window has closed, and what you are actually buying.

The problem, in one paragraph

Concrete is full of tiny interconnected pores. Water soaks into them. When that water freezes it expands by roughly nine percent, and because it is inside the material, it pushes outward on the surrounding concrete. Thaw, refreeze, repeat. Every cycle widens the pores slightly and weakens the bond at the surface.

The visible result is spalling: the surface flaking, pitting and crumbling away in patches. Once it starts, it accelerates, because the damaged surface holds even more water.

Why Idaho is harder on concrete than people expect

Our winters are not especially severe by national standards. What matters here is not how cold it gets but how often it crosses zero.

A place that drops below freezing in November and stays there until March puts concrete through one long freeze. The Treasure Valley instead swings: cold nights, thawing afternoons, over and over through the season. That is far more damaging, because each cycle is a fresh expansion event.

Add in strong high-desert UV that dries and degrades the surface through summer, hard water leaving mineral deposits, and deicing salt tracked off the roads in winter, and unsealed concrete here has a harder life than its appearance suggests.

When sealing is absolutely worth it

On new concrete. This is the clearest case. A new driveway or patio should generally be sealed once it has fully cured, which usually means waiting around a month. Sealing a slab before it has ever been through a winter means it never starts the damage cycle. If you are in one of the newer South Meridian, South Nampa or Harris Ranch builds and your concrete is a year or two old, this is the single best maintenance decision available to you.

On sound older concrete. If the surface is solid, with no significant spalling, sealing after a thorough clean will slow further deterioration considerably.

On decorative or stamped concrete. These depend on a sealer to protect the colour, and the sealer is consumable. Resealing on schedule is not optional maintenance, it is how the finish survives.

On pavers. Sealing stabilises joint sand, locks in colour and reduces weed growth between units.

Anywhere you park vehicles. Oil and transmission fluid soak straight into unsealed concrete and are extremely difficult to remove afterward. A sealed slab gives you time to wipe a spill before it becomes a permanent mark.

When it is not the answer

Already badly spalling. Sealer is not a repair product. Sealing over a failing surface locks moisture into damaged concrete and can accelerate the problem. Significant spalling needs resurfacing or replacement, and that is a concrete contractor's job rather than ours.

Concrete that has not cured. Sealing too early traps moisture in the slab. Patience here matters.

A dirty surface. Sealing over staining permanently encapsulates it. Whatever is on the slab when you seal is what you are looking at for the next several years, so cleaning first is not an upsell, it is a requirement.

Where drainage is the real problem. If water is pooling because of grading or a blocked downspout, sealing treats a symptom. Fix the water first.

Penetrating or topical

Worth understanding, because the two behave completely differently.

Penetrating sealers soak into the concrete and react chemically inside the pores. They do not change how the surface looks or feels and they do not peel, because there is no film sitting on top. They give excellent freeze-thaw protection and are usually the right choice for a plain driveway where you want protection rather than appearance.

Topical sealers form a film on the surface. They can add sheen, deepen colour and give better stain resistance, which is why they are standard on decorative and stamped work. The trade-off is that a film can wear unevenly, and in winter it can be slick, so an additive for traction matters on walkways and steps.

For most Treasure Valley driveways, penetrating is the sensible default. For stamped patios and anything where appearance is the point, topical.

What the process actually looks like

Clean thoroughly. A full pressure wash to remove dirt, organic growth and surface contamination.

Treat the stains specifically. Oil needs a degreaser and dwell time. Rust and mineral bleed need their own treatment. This matters because sealing makes anything left behind permanent.

Let it dry completely. Concrete needs to be genuinely dry through the surface layer, not just dry to the touch. This usually means a day or more of suitable weather, which is why scheduling matters and why this is poor work to rush.

Apply evenly. Coverage rate is specified by the product, and over-application is as much a problem as under-application, particularly with topical sealers, which can cloud or peel if laid on too heavily.

Cure before use. Foot traffic and vehicle traffic have different wait times. Driving on a sealer too early marks it.

The whole sequence is weather-dependent, which is the main scheduling constraint. Spring and early autumn are ideal here: warm enough to cure properly, not so hot that the product flashes off before it penetrates.

How often

Penetrating sealers generally last several years, often five or more depending on traffic and exposure.

Topical sealers wear faster, particularly in traffic lanes and anywhere getting full sun, and typically need redoing every two to three years.

The practical test is simple: pour a cup of water on the slab. If it beads and sits, the sealer is working. If it soaks straight in and darkens the concrete, protection is gone.

Staining, while you are at it

Sealing is protection. Staining is appearance, and the two are usually done together because the preparation is the same.

Decorative stain penetrates and colours the concrete rather than coating it, so it does not chip or peel the way paint does. It is the most cost-effective way to turn a plain grey slab into something that looks deliberate, and it is particularly effective on older concrete where the colour has gone patchy with age.

Stain then seal is the normal order, and the sealer is what protects the colour from UV.

Deicing salt deserves its own warning

If you salt your driveway in winter, or if you simply drive on treated roads and park at home, this applies to you.

Deicing chemicals accelerate concrete damage in two ways. They increase the number of freeze-thaw cycles the slab goes through, because they allow melting and refreezing at temperatures where the concrete would otherwise have stayed frozen. And some formulations chemically attack the cement paste directly.

Salt tracked off the road, dripping off a vehicle onto the slab underneath, concentrates in exactly the spot you park. It is a common reason an otherwise sound driveway has one deteriorating patch.

A sealed surface does not make the slab immune, but it substantially reduces how much of that solution soaks in. If you want one practical argument for sealing in this climate, this is it.

What to ask before you hire anyone

  • Which type of sealer are you using, penetrating or topical, and why that one here?
  • What is your plan for the oil stains and the rust?
  • How long after cleaning will you wait before sealing?
  • What is the cure time before I can walk on it and before I can park on it?
  • Is traction additive included on the walkways and steps?
  • How long should this last before it needs redoing?

The answer to the second question is the one that separates a thorough job from a quick one. Sealing over a stain makes it permanent, and that is not something you can undo later.

A note on timing your project

This work is weather-dependent in a way most exterior cleaning is not.

The slab must be genuinely dry before sealer goes down, which after a thorough clean means waiting for suitable weather rather than working to a convenient date. Too cold and the product will not cure properly. Too hot and it can flash off the surface before penetrating.

Spring and early autumn are the reliable windows here. Both get busy, and both are short, so if you are planning to seal this year it is worth getting on a schedule early rather than calling during the first cold snap.

If you are in a new build and the concrete was poured recently, note the cure time too. Sealing a slab that has not finished curing traps moisture inside it, which is worse than not sealing at all.

Common questions

Will sealing make my driveway slippery? A penetrating sealer will not, because there is no film. A topical sealer can, which is why a traction additive matters on walkways, steps and any slope.

Will it change the colour? Penetrating sealers are essentially invisible. Topical sealers usually darken concrete slightly and add some sheen, which many people prefer on a patio and fewer prefer on a plain driveway.

Can I seal over an existing sealer? Sometimes, if it is the same type and still sound. Mixing types, particularly putting a topical over a penetrating, often fails. Knowing what is already on there matters.

How do I know what is already on there? The water test. If water beads, something is working. If it soaks in, you are effectively unsealed.

Do I need to reseal the whole thing or just the worn parts? Whole surface. Spot sealing is visible, because the treated and untreated areas weather differently from that point on.

Is it worth the money?

The arithmetic is straightforward. Replacing a driveway is a major expense. Sealing it periodically is a small fraction of that, and it directly addresses the mechanism that causes the failure.

The case is strongest on new concrete, where you are preventing damage that has not started, and weakest on concrete that is already deteriorating, where you may be too late to do much good.

If your slab is sound and under about ten years old, this is one of the better-value things you can do to it.

How to Get Rid of Black Streaks on Your Roof

Those dark streaks are a living organism feeding on your shingles, not dirt. What they are, why they appear on some slopes and not others, and the only method that removes them without damage.

Richard Prins · 2026-09-01 · 8 min read · 1837 words · /blog/black-streaks-on-roof

If your roof has dark vertical streaks running down it, you are not looking at dirt, soot, or weathering. You are looking at something alive, and it is feeding on the roof.

The good news is that it is straightforward to remove. The bad news is that the obvious way to remove it is also the way people destroy their roofs.

What the streaks actually are

The organism is Gloeocapsa magma, a type of blue-green algae. It travels on the wind as airborne spores, lands on a roof, and if conditions suit it, takes hold.

What makes asphalt shingles so hospitable is the limestone filler used in their manufacture. The algae feeds on it. That is why it colonises shingle roofs specifically and tends to leave metal and tile alone.

The dark colour is a protective sheath the organism develops against UV. It is effectively sunscreen, and it is what makes the colony visible from the street.

The streaking runs vertically because rain carries spores and nutrients downslope. That is also why the staining is usually worst below the ridge and heaviest where several planes drain together.

Why one slope and not the others

Almost always because of moisture and sun.

North-facing slopes stay damp the longest. They get the least direct sun, dry slowest after rain or overnight dew, and therefore offer the most hospitable conditions. In most Treasure Valley homes the north pitch goes first, sometimes by years.

Shade does the same thing regardless of orientation. The mature canopy over the North End and Hyde Park, the older streets of Nampa and Caldwell under their elms, and anywhere with large trees close to the house all keep roof surfaces damp enough for growth that a bare lot would not support.

Proximity to water does it too. Homes backing the Boise River and the Greenbelt, through Harris Ranch and Barber Valley, sit in ambient humidity that does not fully burn off even on hot days.

Worth saying clearly: we are a semi-arid climate at roughly ten to twelve inches of rain a year. The heavy, carpet-like roof moss common in the Pacific Northwest is not typical here. What we get is algae streaking, which is widespread, and localised moss in genuinely shaded, damp pockets. Anyone telling you every roof in the valley is covered in moss is selling rather than looking.

Is it actually damaging the roof?

Yes, though more slowly than a sales pitch might suggest. Three mechanisms:

Moisture retention. The colony holds water against the shingle surface rather than letting it dry. Prolonged dampness degrades the asphalt and accelerates granule loss.

Heat absorption. A dark roof absorbs more solar energy than a light one. Heavy streaking measurably raises attic temperatures, which both ages the shingle from underneath and shows up in cooling costs.

Moss, where it follows. Algae is the mild version. Moss is the genuinely destructive one, because it roots. As it establishes along shingle edges it lifts them, and a lifted shingle lets water underneath. That is how algae streaking turns into an actual leak.

The realistic framing is that this does not cause a sudden failure. It shortens the life of the roof, quietly, over years.

The thing you must not do

Do not pressure wash it.

Asphalt shingles are protected by a layer of mineral granules embedded in the surface. Those granules shield the asphalt from UV, and UV is what destroys asphalt. The granules are, functionally, the roof.

High pressure removes them. Not all at once and not visibly from the ground, but you will find them in the gutters afterward, and the shingle underneath ages dramatically faster from that point.

Three further consequences worth knowing:

  • Nearly every shingle manufacturer voids the warranty on a pressure-washed roof
  • A home inspector or roofer can identify it, which matters at resale
  • High pressure on a slope can force water up under the shingle courses

It will look clean the day it is done. That is the problem: the damage is invisible and the result is immediate, so people recommend it to each other.

The method that works

Soft washing. Low pressure, roughly what comes out of a garden hose, combined with a cleaning solution that kills the organism.

The solution does the work. It penetrates the colony and kills it at the root rather than stripping the visible layer off the surface. The low-pressure rinse then carries away the dead material.

The sequence that matters:

  1. Inspect the roof material, pitch and condition, and flag anything damaged before starting
  2. Protect everything underneath, saturating planting with plain water first so it absorbs far less
  3. Apply at low pressure, working so the solution flows down the slope
  4. Dwell, which is the step that decides whether the clean lasts
  5. Rinse gently and re-water the planting
  6. Check the gutters, because a lot of dead material ends up in them

Dwell time is where corners get cut. Spraying and immediately rinsing removes the colour and leaves the organism, which is why some roofs are visibly streaking again within a few months.

Can you do this yourself?

Technically. Realistically, this is the service I would most strongly suggest not doing yourself, for reasons that have nothing to do with wanting the work.

The hazard is height. Roof falls are among the most common serious home-maintenance injuries, and a wet roof with cleaning solution on it is considerably more slippery than a dry one.

The second issue is everything underneath. The solution that kills algae will also kill planting if it is not pre-wet and rinsed, and the runoff goes exactly where your downspouts send it.

The third is concentration. Too weak does nothing. Too strong can lighten shingles unevenly, which leaves you with a different cosmetic problem and no way back.

If you are going to try it anyway: work from a ladder rather than walking the roof, never on a windy day, pre-wet everything below, and have someone with you.

Stopping it coming back

Zinc or copper strips along the ridge release trace metal ions as rain runs over them, which inhibits growth downslope. Effective, inexpensive, best fitted when a roof is being replaced.

Trim back overhanging branches. More sun and more airflow means faster drying, and faster drying means less hospitable conditions. This also reduces the organic debris that feeds growth.

Keep gutters clear. A blocked gutter holds water at the roof edge, which keeps the lowest course of shingles damp.

Algae-resistant shingles. If you are already replacing the roof, these have copper granules built in. Worth specifying.

Treat it early. A light streak is a quick job. A decade of accumulation with moss established in the valleys is a long one.

How often does it need doing?

In our climate, every two to three years for most homes.

Shaded and north-facing slopes will come back sooner. Open, sunny lots on newer builds in South Meridian or South Nampa may go considerably longer.

If streaking returns within a few months of a clean, that is not bad luck, it is a sign the colony was removed rather than killed.

Telling algae apart from the other things on a roof

Not every dark mark is the same problem, and the treatment differs.

Algae is the flat, dark vertical streaking described above. It sits on the surface with no real thickness and runs downslope. It is the most common thing on Treasure Valley roofs by a wide margin.

Moss is three-dimensional. Green, spongy, and you can see it standing proud of the shingle, usually clustered along edges, in valleys and around penetrations where debris collects. Moss is the one that physically lifts shingles and is worth treating urgently.

Lichen looks like flat, crusty grey-green patches, often roughly circular. It is the most stubborn of the three, because it anchors into the shingle surface. Killing it is straightforward; getting every trace of the anchor point out without damaging the shingle is not, and some shadowing can remain.

Granule loss is not growth at all. It shows as patches where the shingle looks darker and smoother than the surrounding area, because the mineral layer has gone and the asphalt beneath is exposed. Cleaning will not fix it. If you are seeing this alongside a lot of granules in the gutters, the conversation to have is with a roofer rather than with us.

Soot and general grime happens near busy roads and after a heavy wildfire smoke season. It is not organic, it is not feeding on anything, and it rinses far more easily.

What a clean roof is worth at resale

If you are planning to sell, this is one of the highest-return items on the list.

Roof condition is one of the first things a buyer asks about and one of the most expensive things they fear. A roof with heavy black streaking reads as an old roof, even when it has years of life left. That perception shows up in offers and in inspection negotiations.

It also shows up in photography. The roof is a large proportion of the visible surface in most exterior listing photos, particularly aerial and drone shots, which are now standard. Streaking is extremely visible from above.

Cleaning the roof before photographs are taken is a small cost against a large asset, and unlike most pre-listing work it changes the single element buyers worry about most.

Common questions

How long until the streaks disappear? Most of the discolouration lifts during the wash. Some residual shading can continue to fade over the following few weeks as rain and weather carry away the last of the dead material.

Will it come back? Eventually. Spores are airborne and the conditions that favoured growth have not changed. A proper treatment kills what is there, so you restart from zero rather than from surviving roots. Two to three years is typical here.

Can you treat just the bad slope? Yes, though it is worth knowing that the other slopes almost certainly have early growth that is simply not visible yet, and treating everything at once is usually better value than returning.

Does insurance cover this? No. It is maintenance, not damage. The relevant point is the reverse: neglected maintenance can affect a claim if it contributed to a failure.

My roof is only a few years old and already streaking. That is normal in a shaded or north-facing situation. Age is less relevant than exposure.

Will this fix a leak? No. Cleaning is not repair. If you have a leak, you need a roofer first.

The short version

Black roof streaks are algae feeding on the limestone in your shingles. They favour north-facing and shaded slopes, they hold moisture against the roof, and where moss follows they lift shingles and let water in.

Soft washing removes them properly. Pressure washing removes them visibly and takes years off the roof doing it.

What Does Post-Construction Exterior Cleanup Include?

The last ten percent of a build is what a buyer actually sees first. What exterior cleanup covers, who usually pays for it, and why the timing matters more than people expect.

Richard Prins · 2026-08-25 · 8 min read · 1750 words · /blog/what-does-post-construction-cleanup-include

A new build looks finished long before it is actually clean.

The trades pull out, the interior gets its final clean, and the inside looks immaculate. Then someone walks up the driveway and sees concrete dust on the flatwork, drywall haze on the windows, mortar smears on the brick and paint flecks across the siding.

That is the first impression of an expensive project, and it is the cheapest part of the whole job to get right.

What is actually on a new build

The residue from construction is not ordinary dirt, which is why ordinary cleaning does not shift it.

Mortar and grout smears. Cementitious residue bonds chemically to brick, stone and concrete. It does not dissolve in water and it does not come off with pressure alone. It needs a specific treatment, correctly diluted for the substrate, or you will etch what is underneath.

Stucco and render splatter. Same problem, usually over a wider area and often onto surfaces nobody protected.

Paint overspray. Fine mist from spraying carries further than people expect. It lands on glass, hardware, trim and whatever was parked nearby, and it needs mechanical removal with the right tools rather than solvent that will damage frames.

Drywall and silica dust. Extremely fine, it gets everywhere, and it turns to a stubborn film the moment it is wet. Hosing it off a window without treating it first just spreads it.

Adhesive residue. Labels and protective film left on glass and fixtures, particularly if they have been baking in the sun for a few weeks, leave adhesive that needs solvent work.

Caulk and sealant smears. Silicone on glass is its own small nightmare and needs careful mechanical removal.

Tyre marks and oil. From deliveries and site traffic across the new flatwork.

And then high-desert dust on top of all of it. Every Treasure Valley site is exposed, and much of the current building is on open ground at the edge of town, out toward South Meridian, South Nampa, the Caldwell subdivisions and the foothills above Eagle. Wind puts a layer over everything, and strong UV bakes it on.

What the service covers

A complete exterior cleanup normally includes:

Hard surfaces washed down. Siding, soffits, fascia, and any masonry, with the method matched to the material.

Flatwork cleaned. Driveway, paths, patio and garage apron, which means treating mortar residue and tyre marks, not just rinsing.

Glass detailed. Overspray, adhesive, labels and construction film removed, plus frames and tracks cleared of the grit that accumulates during a build.

Debris cleared. Offcuts, packaging, fixings and the general scatter that ends up in beds and along fence lines.

Gutters checked and flushed. Roofing work puts a surprising amount into a new gutter system before the house has seen a single storm.

Fixtures and hardware wiped down. Light fittings, house numbers, handrails and meter boxes.

What it does not include: interior cleaning, landscaping, or repairs. Those are separate trades.

Why the method matters more here than anywhere else

Every surface on a new build is new, which means every mistake is a mistake on something that has never been used.

Fresh concrete has not fully cured and will not tolerate aggressive pressure. New stucco marks easily. Factory finishes on siding, window frames and garage doors are exactly the coatings that high pressure strips. Newly installed glass is the most expensive thing to scratch.

So the right approach is real pressure where a hard surface genuinely needs it, and a gentle soft wash on anything delicate or newly finished. A company running one setting over the whole envelope will leave marks on something that was installed last week.

Sequence matters too. The work goes from the roofline down, so that dust rinsed off a higher surface does not settle onto something already cleaned. Doing it in the wrong order means doing parts of it twice.

When to schedule it

After every trade is finished and before the final walkthrough, handover or photography. That window is usually narrower than anyone plans for.

The common mistake is booking it too early. If exterior cleanup happens before the last trade has been back to finish a punch item, you pay for it twice. One more person on site with a caulk gun or a paint pot undoes a surprising amount.

The second mistake is booking it too late. Realtors need photographs, buyers need a walkthrough, and both tend to be scheduled before anyone thinks about the driveway.

For a listing, exterior cleanup should be done before photography, not after. The photographs are what most buyers see first, and they last far longer than the open house.

Who pays for it

It varies, and it is worth settling in writing early.

On a custom build, it is often the general contractor's responsibility as part of final handover, though it is sometimes excluded and left with the owner. Read the contract rather than assuming.

On a spec build, the builder usually handles it, because they are selling the result.

On a remodel, it is frequently nobody's line item, which is exactly why it is so often skipped. A remodel generates the same mortar dust and overspray as a new build and rarely has a cleanup allowance attached.

For owners taking possession, it is worth asking directly whether exterior cleanup is included before closing. It is much easier to negotiate beforehand than to chase afterward.

Residential and commercial are different jobs

For a house, the scope is the building envelope and the flatwork, and the deadline is usually a walkthrough.

For commercial, there is more of it and the constraints are different. Larger glazed areas, more hardstanding, parking lots with striping that may need protecting, and loading areas that have taken heavy traffic. Access is often restricted to outside business hours, and there is usually a tenant or a certificate-of-occupancy deadline driving the schedule.

Industrial pads, like the newer buildings out at Sky Ranch and the North Ranch business parks in Caldwell, or the commercial frontages along the Nampa corridors, generally need the same treatment at a larger scale.

What to ask for in a quote

  • Which surfaces are included, specifically
  • Whether mortar and overspray removal is included or extra
  • What method will be used on new stucco, new concrete and factory finishes
  • Whether glass is included inside, outside, or both
  • Whether gutters are included
  • What happens if a trade returns and remarks something
  • Proof of insurance, which matters more on an active site than almost anywhere else

That last point is not a formality. A site with other contractors still moving through it is exactly where liability questions arise.

A realistic sequence for a handover

For anyone coordinating this, the order that avoids paying twice:

  1. All trades complete, including punch-list returns. This is the step that slips. One more visit with a paint pot undoes the exterior clean.
  2. Interior final clean. Doing interiors after exteriors means tracking dust back out.
  3. Exterior cleanup. Roofline down to the driveway.
  4. Landscaping and final grading. Anything involving soil after the hard surfaces are clean means a light re-rinse, so some coordinators prefer landscaping before step three and accept a touch-up.
  5. Photography.
  6. Walkthrough or handover.

The two orderings that cause the most rework are photography before cleanup and landscaping after it.

What goes wrong when it is skipped

It rarely stays skipped. It just gets dealt with later, by someone else, at greater expense.

Mortar residue hardens. Left for months it becomes substantially harder to remove, and the treatment required becomes more aggressive, on brick that is now someone's house.

Overspray cures. Fresh overspray on glass comes off reasonably easily. After a summer of UV it is considerably more work, and the risk of scratching the glass during removal goes up.

Construction dust gets rained into the surface. Fine silica washed into new concrete does not simply rinse away later.

The buyer notices. This is the real cost. A punch list that includes "clean the exterior" at the end of a project signals a lack of care about everything that preceded it.

If you are a builder or a remodeler

A few things worth knowing from the cleaning side.

Bundling helps. If you are putting up several homes in the same subdivision, scheduling them together is substantially cheaper per property than booking each one separately, because the setup and the travel are shared. Most of the current activity is concentrated, so this is usually practical.

Timing can be reserved. The crunch always arrives at the same point in a project, and the exterior clean tends to be the thing everyone remembers last. Booking a provisional slot against your expected completion date, and moving it as the date moves, costs nothing and avoids a scramble.

Protecting surfaces during the build is cheaper than cleaning them afterward. Obvious, routinely skipped, and the single biggest lever on what the final clean costs.

Common questions

How long does it take? A typical new home is most of a day. Larger properties and commercial buildings take longer, and access restrictions can stretch the schedule across more than one visit.

Do you need water and power on site? Water, ideally. If the supply is not connected yet, say so when booking, because it changes the equipment needed.

Can you work around other trades? Yes, though it is rarely the best value. Anything cleaned while another trade is still working is likely to need attention again.

Is this different from a regular house wash? Substantially. A house wash deals with accumulated dirt and organic growth. Post-construction deals with bonded cementitious residue, overspray and adhesive, which are different problems needing different treatments.

Do you do the windows inside as well? Exterior glass is standard. Interiors can be included, and on a new build it is usually worth it, because drywall haze does not care which side of the glass it lands on.

Why it is worth doing properly

The whole argument is proportion. Exterior cleanup is a small fraction of the cost of any build or remodel, and it is the part that determines what the finished project actually looks like to the first person who sees it.

A buyer walking up a driveway with mortar dust on it and haze on the windows does not think "that will clean up." They think the work was rushed. Fair or not, it colours everything they look at afterward.

How Often Should You Wash Your House in Boise?

Once a year suits most Treasure Valley homes, but your lot decides more than the calendar does. How to read your own property, and what waiting too long actually costs.

Richard Prins · 2026-08-18 · 8 min read · 1799 words · /blog/how-often-should-you-wash-your-house

The short answer is once a year for most Treasure Valley homes, in spring.

The useful answer is that your lot matters far more than the calendar. Two identical houses three streets apart can need genuinely different schedules, and the difference is almost always about shade, water and what is upwind.

What actually accumulates on a house here

Four things, and they behave differently.

High-desert dust. Constant through the dry months. It is inorganic, it does not grow, and it rinses off reasonably well. It is also relentless, and it works into textured surfaces like stucco far more than it does into smooth siding.

Organic growth. Algae and mildew on anything that stays damp. This is the one that genuinely needs treating rather than rinsing, because it has roots and it comes back from them.

Hard water deposits. From sprinkler overspray hitting siding and glass. Mineral, not dirt, and it builds up permanently until something dissolves it.

Pollen and seed. Seasonal, heavy in spring, mostly cosmetic.

A house with only the first and the fourth needs a wash. A house with the second and third needs a treatment, which is a different job.

Once a year, and why spring

Spring works for most homes for three practical reasons.

Winter leaves the worst of it behind: grime that has sat since autumn, salt and road film near busy streets, and whatever the freeze-thaw cycle has driven into porous surfaces.

Doing it before the dry season starts means the house looks right through the months you are actually outside, and it gives you a clean baseline before the dust arrives.

And it pairs naturally with the other spring jobs: gutters after winter, concrete before sealing season, windows before you start opening them.

When once a year is not enough

You have significant tree cover

This is the biggest single factor. Shade keeps surfaces damp, damp grows algae, and trees drop organic debris that feeds it.

The mature canopy through the North End and Hyde Park is the clearest example in Boise. Old Town Meridian near Kleiner Park, the elm-shaded streets of the Caldwell historic districts, and Old Nampa all behave the same way. Those homes often want attention twice a year, and the north elevation usually goes first regardless of what the rest of the house looks like.

You are near water

Homes backing the Boise River and the Greenbelt, through Harris Ranch and Barber Valley, and around the private lakes in Eagle communities like Two Rivers and Laguna Pointe, sit in ambient humidity that does not burn off the way it does on an open lot.

Your sprinklers hit the house

Extremely common and almost entirely fixable. Overspray hitting siding and glass every morning through an irrigation season produces mineral staining that compounds year over year. Adjusting the heads is ten minutes of work and the highest-value maintenance decision on this list.

You are on the agricultural edge

Homes out toward Lake Lowell, along the farm corridors, out past the Caldwell vineyards or on acreage near open ground deal with field dust and crop debris that suburban lots do not. The load is simply higher.

You are near active construction

A lot of the valley is. Jobsite dust travels, and it is finer and stickier than ordinary dust.

It is a commercial frontage

Different standard entirely. A storefront is usually quarterly or monthly, because it is the first thing a customer sees.

When once a year is more than enough

Open lot, full sun on all elevations, no large trees, sprinklers correctly adjusted, not near farmland or construction. That describes a lot of newer South Meridian, South Nampa and outer subdivision housing.

Those homes genuinely can go eighteen months to two years between washes without looking neglected, and there is no sense paying for a schedule the property does not need.

How to read your own house

Rather than guessing, go and look. Three checks.

Walk the north side. Whatever is going to happen to your house happens there first. If the north elevation has a green or grey film, or dark streaking under the eaves, you are overdue regardless of what the front looks like.

Look at the sheltered spots. Under eaves, behind downspouts, around the base of walls, anywhere that does not get direct sun or rain. Growth starts in the protected places.

Check the glass near the sprinklers. Cloudy spotting at the bottom of windows on the lawn side tells you overspray is hitting the house, and if it is on the glass it is on the siding too.

If all three look fine, you can wait. If the north side is going, book it.

What waiting too long actually costs

The case is not purely cosmetic.

Organic growth holds moisture against the building. On wood siding and trim, prolonged dampness is how rot starts. On stucco, it works into the texture and becomes much harder to remove.

Mineral staining becomes permanent. Deposits that sit through repeated heat cycles bond harder, and on glass they eventually etch. The same stain is easy this year and may be permanent in five.

Growth that establishes needs stronger treatment. A light film is a straightforward soft wash. Years of accumulation needs more dwell time, more product and more care, which costs more.

Paint and finishes degrade faster under a layer that traps moisture against them.

The pattern across all of them is the same: regular is cheaper per visit than occasional, because you never let the expensive problems establish.

What a house wash should actually involve

Worth knowing, because "house wash" covers a wide range.

A proper job is a soft wash on the siding, which means low pressure and a cleaning solution that kills organic growth rather than blasting it. High pressure on siding forces water behind panels and into the wall cavity, which is a far more expensive problem than a dirty house.

It should include the soffits, fascia and eaves, which is where growth hides and where most of the dark streaking lives.

Landscaping gets pre-wet and rinsed.

It usually makes sense to do the flatwork at the same visit, because the setup is shared and the driveway is what people actually look at on their way to the door.

Siding material changes the answer

What your house is clad in affects both how often it needs washing and how it has to be done.

Vinyl is the most forgiving and the most common on newer builds. It does not absorb much, so dirt sits on the surface and releases easily. The risk is pressure, which forces water up behind the panels, because vinyl is designed to shed water downward and is not sealed at the laps.

Stucco is porous and textured, which means it holds dust in a way smooth siding does not, and it shows organic growth more obviously. It also cannot take pressure, because the texture chips. Stucco homes often look like they need washing sooner than they actually do, and they need a gentler approach when they do.

Fibre cement is durable and takes cleaning well, but the paint finish is the thing you are protecting, so soft washing is the right method.

Wood and cedar are the most demanding. They absorb moisture, they gouge under pressure, and the grain raises if they are treated roughly. Older homes through the North End, Hyde Park, Old Town Eagle and the historic Nampa and Caldwell districts frequently have original wood siding and trim that needs the gentlest handling on this list.

Brick is low maintenance but the mortar is not. Aggressive cleaning washes out soft or aged mortar joints, which is a repointing job.

What this looks like through the year

Late winter into spring. The main event. House wash, gutters cleared of whatever winter left, windows, and the flatwork. This is also when you want to be booking concrete sealing, because the weather window for curing is narrow and it fills.

Early summer. Walk the irrigation zones and watch where the heads actually throw. This single habit prevents more staining than any cleaning schedule.

Late summer. The dust peak. Mostly a question of whether appearance matters to you between now and autumn. Commercial frontages usually need attention here.

Autumn. Gutters, before the first hard freeze, and this window is tighter than people expect. Roof cleaning if it is due, while the weather still cooperates.

Winter. Not a cleaning season here, but it is when freeze and thaw does its work on unsealed concrete, and it is the argument for having sealed it in spring.

Common questions

Can I just do it myself with a pressure washer? On concrete, yes, with care. On siding, this is where most DIY damage happens, because the instinct is to use more pressure when something is not coming off, and on siding more pressure is exactly wrong. If the house is dirty rather than growing something, a garden hose and a soft brush achieve more than people expect.

Does a house wash include the roof? Usually not. They are different jobs on different schedules, and the roof is every two to three years rather than annually. They do pair well in one visit if both are due.

Will washing damage my paint? A soft wash should not. High pressure absolutely can, particularly on older or already-failing paint.

What about the gutters' exterior? The dark vertical streaking on the outside of gutters is a specific deposit that does not simply rinse off. It is usually handled as part of a house wash rather than as part of clearing the channels, so ask whether it is included.

I am selling. Is this worth doing? Yes, and it is one of the better-value items on the pre-listing list. Exterior photographs are what buyers see first, and a clean house, clean driveway and streak-free roof change the impression considerably for a small outlay.

A sensible schedule for most homes

  • Spring: house wash, gutters cleared after winter, windows
  • Autumn: gutters again, before the first hard freeze
  • Every two to three years: roof soft wash
  • Every few years: concrete sealing, depending on the product and the traffic

That covers the majority of Treasure Valley properties. Adjust upward for shade, water, sprinklers and farmland, and downward for an open, sunny lot.

The short version

Once a year in spring is right for most homes here. Go to twice if you have real tree cover, sit near water, or have sprinklers hitting the building. Stretch it if you are on an open lot with full sun.

The north elevation is your indicator. Check that, not the front.

Graffiti Removal for Boise Businesses: What to Do in the First 48 Hours

A tag that sits invites the next one. What to do immediately, why the surface decides the method, and why painting over it is usually the worst option available.

Richard Prins · 2026-08-11 · 8 min read · 1815 words · /blog/graffiti-removal-boise-businesses

If you arrive to find a tag on your building, the single most useful thing to know is that speed matters more than anything else you will decide today.

Not because the paint gets harder to remove, though it does. Because a tag that stays up is an advertisement that nobody is paying attention.

Why the first 48 hours matter

Tagging is territorial and it is social. A visible tag that remains visible communicates two things to whoever comes past next: this wall is available, and nothing happens to people who use it.

Property managers and city programmes across the country converge on the same conclusion, which is that rapid removal is the most effective single deterrent available. A wall cleared within a day or two tends to get hit less often than one where tags accumulate.

The practical version: treat it as urgent even though it feels cosmetic.

There is a second reason, which is chemical. Spray paint continues to cure after application. Fresh paint sits more on the surface and releases more readily. Paint that has baked through a few days of Idaho summer sun has worked further into a porous substrate and needs more aggressive treatment.

What to do before anyone touches it

Four things, in this order.

Photograph it. Wide shot showing the location, then close-ups. You need this for insurance, for a police report, and for your own records if it becomes a pattern.

Report it. File a police report even if you expect nothing to come of it. Repeat incidents at the same address build a case, and some insurance policies require a report before they will consider a claim.

Check your policy. Many commercial property policies cover vandalism, including graffiti removal. Whether it is worth claiming depends on your deductible, but it is worth knowing before you pay out of pocket.

Do not start scrubbing. This is the one that causes damage. The wrong product on the wrong surface sets the pigment permanently or ruins the substrate, and once that has happened a professional cannot undo it.

The surface decides everything

This is the part that determines whether removal works or leaves a permanent mark.

Porous brick and masonry. The hardest case. Brick is full of voids and the paint wicks into them. Removal needs a poultice-type remover that draws pigment back out, plus dwell time. Pressure alone fails and will erode the mortar joints, which on the older brick through downtown Boise, Old Boise and the historic districts of Nampa and Caldwell is a genuine conservation issue.

Unsealed concrete. Similar, slightly more forgiving, and able to take more pressure once the remover has done its work.

Painted walls. Here the challenge is reversed. The remover that lifts the graffiti will usually lift the underlying paint too, because chemically they are not that different. Often the realistic outcome is removal plus a touch-up in the original colour.

Metal, glass and coated surfaces. Usually the easiest. Paint sits on top rather than soaking in. Glass can often be cleared mechanically. Etched glass, where acid has been used, is a different and much worse problem, because the glass itself is damaged and only replacement truly fixes it.

Stucco and EIFS. Awkward. Porous and texturally delicate, so aggressive cleaning destroys the finish. Common along the commercial stretches on Cleveland Boulevard in Caldwell and the Nampa retail corridors.

Vinyl siding. Solvents can soften or discolour it, so product choice is narrow.

The point of listing these is simple: anyone who proposes the same approach for brick and for vinyl has not looked at your building.

Why painting over it is usually the wrong call

It is the instinct, and it is understandable, because it is fast and cheap on the day.

What goes wrong:

Colour never matches. Even the same tin, years later, has weathered differently. You get a visible rectangle, which reads as "there was graffiti here" just as clearly as the tag did.

Patches accumulate. After three incidents the wall is a patchwork, and the building looks worse than if it had been cleaned each time.

It can bleed through. Some spray paints ghost through ordinary coatings unless a blocking primer is used.

It signals tolerance. A painted patch is visibly a response to graffiti. Clean masonry is not.

Painting is the right answer in one scenario: the wall was painted already, the damage is extensive, and a full-elevation repaint in the correct colour is genuinely the most economical route. Full elevation, not a patch.

What professional removal involves

Identify the substrate and test. Always in an inconspicuous spot first. Media, paint type and substrate interact in ways you cannot reliably predict from looking.

Select the method. Chemical removers for porous masonry, controlled pressure where the surface will take it, specialist approaches for sensitive and historic materials.

Protect the surroundings. Windows, fixtures, planting and anything downstream of the runoff.

Work in stages. Several gentle passes beat one aggressive one. Aggressive is what leaves a clean patch that stands out as much as the tag did.

Neutralise and rinse. Residue left on masonry keeps working on it.

Blend the result. This is the detail that separates a good job from an adequate one. Cleaning a one-metre square of a dirty wall perfectly leaves a bright patch. Feathering the clean outward so the area blends is what makes the repair disappear.

Preventing the next one

Remove fast, every time. The most effective measure by a distance.

Anti-graffiti coating. A sacrificial or permanent clear coat means the next tag washes off rather than soaking in. Worth it on walls that have been hit more than once, and the cost is recovered quickly if you are a repeat target.

Lighting. Tagging happens where it cannot be seen happening.

Sightlines. Dense planting and blank service alleys create the conditions. Opening up a sightline often does more than a camera.

Murals. Genuinely effective. Walls carrying commissioned art are tagged markedly less than blank ones. Boise has a real culture around this, and Freak Alley is the city's sanctioned mural gallery, which is also a useful thing to be able to point to: art belongs there, and the surrounding private walls are not an extension of it.

Where this happens around the valley

Downtown Boise carries the most, through BoDo, Old Boise, around The Grove and the Basque Block, where storefront frontages are the exposed surface.

The busy corridors take their share: State Street, Broadway near Boise State, and the commercial stretches along Fairview, Overland and Vista.

Out in the valley the pattern shifts to retail and industrial. The Eagle Road corridor around The Village, the Caldwell Boulevard and Karcher retail spine in Nampa, District 208, the Garrity industrial area, and the business parks at Sky Ranch and North Ranch in Caldwell.

Each of those is a different substrate problem. Historic brick downtown, stucco and EIFS on the retail strips, painted metal and block on the industrial pads.

The cost of leaving it up

Worth being concrete about why this is urgent rather than annoying.

It attracts more. The single clearest effect. Tags accumulate on walls that tolerate them.

It changes how customers read the business. A storefront with a tag on it signals neglect, regardless of how well run the business is. People make that judgement before they reach the door and they are not conscious of making it.

It affects tenants and renewals. If you manage property, visible vandalism is something tenants raise, and it comes up at renewal.

It can breach a lease or an HOA standard. Many commercial leases put the obligation on the tenant, with a time limit. Worth checking who is actually responsible before assuming it is the landlord.

It compounds. Each incident is cheaper to deal with individually than three at once, partly because of mobilisation and partly because fresher paint releases more easily.

If you are a repeat target

Some addresses get hit repeatedly, and at that point the economics change.

Rapid removal is still the foundation, but after two or three incidents an anti-graffiti coating usually pays for itself. There are two broad types. Sacrificial coatings wash away with the graffiti and are reapplied afterward; they are cheaper and well suited to porous masonry. Permanent coatings stay in place through multiple cleanings and suit smoother, less absorbent substrates.

Either way, the next tag comes off with far less effort and far less risk to the wall underneath, which matters most on historic brick where aggressive removal is not an option.

It is also worth getting on a standing arrangement rather than calling each time. For a building that is hit regularly, a scheduled check with removal included is both faster in response and cheaper per incident than reactive callouts.

Common questions

How quickly can it be removed? For most surfaces, same or next day is realistic. The variable is substrate: historic brick needs testing and often a poultice with dwell time, so it may be a two-visit job.

Will the wall look the same afterward? On most surfaces, yes, if the clean is feathered outward so there is no bright patch. On very porous brick that has been tagged repeatedly, some shadowing can remain. Anyone promising a perfect result on old brick before seeing it is guessing.

Will insurance cover it? Often, under vandalism cover. Whether it is worth claiming depends on your deductible and on how it affects your premium. File the police report either way.

What about etched glass? Acid etching physically damages the glass. Light etching can sometimes be polished, but in most cases the honest answer is replacement.

Can you remove it from a historic building? Yes, and it needs the gentlest approach of anything on this list. Pressure erodes aged mortar, and that damage is not reversible. This is the case where method matters most.

Do you work out of hours? For commercial frontages, usually the sensible option, so the work happens before opening rather than in front of customers.

What to ask a removal company

  • Have you worked on this substrate before?
  • Will you test an inconspicuous area first?
  • What happens if the paint does not fully lift?
  • Will the cleaned area blend, or will it be a bright patch?
  • Are you insured, and can I see proof?
  • Do you offer an anti-graffiti coating afterward?
  • How quickly can you get here?
